Field guide · October 2026

The Media Industry Map

Media is one business that keeps changing its shape: make something people pay attention to, then sell that attention or turn it into a sale. The printing press, the penny newspaper, TV and TikTok all run on it. If you understand that loop, you can build a machine that runs it for brands.

One correction up front. By dollars, media isn't the biggest consumer industry. Food, housing and healthcare are all bigger, and global media and entertainment runs about $3 trillion a year. What media does win is time. It's the biggest claim on a consumer's waking hours, and every other industry has to buy its way in through it. That makes it the most important industry for a brand to understand.

1 · Specialized knowledge

Your edge is where the skills overlap

Being world-class at one thing is rare and slow. Being in the top 25% at three things that almost nobody combines is achievable, and the combination is what's rare. Scott Adams called this a "talent stack". Most people call it T-shaped: wide across many areas, deep in one.

How skills actually get learned

Deliberate practice

Anders Ericsson's research: experts improve by working just past their current ability, with fast, specific feedback. Repetition alone doesn't do it.

Your version of it

Read output → tighten prompt

Every teardown you read and every prompt rule you fix is a rep. The machine gives you instant feedback at a volume no copywriter ever had. That loop is your deliberate practice.

The trap

Knowing without deciding

Reading theory feels like progress. Each thing you learn should turn into a rule in a prompt within a week, or it's trivia.

2 · How it began

570 years, one business model

Orange marks the turns that changed the money. Each one made content cheaper to produce or distribute, and each one handed more power to whoever controlled distribution.

Print era
1450

Gutenberg's press

Copies become cheap. Publishing is born as a business: pay up front to make the thing, sell many copies.

1605

First newspapers

Regular, dated news sold by subscription to merchants who needed information. Paid for by readers, not advertisers.

1833

The penny press

The New York Sun sells for a penny, below the cost of printing it. The money comes from advertisers buying the crowd. This is the founding moment of modern media: the reader becomes the product.

1869

First ad agency

N.W. Ayer & Son starts buying space and writing ads for brands. Advertising becomes a profession.

1890s

Mass magazines

Cyrus Curtis's Ladies' Home Journal reaches a million readers. National brands (Ivory, Kodak, Coca-Cola) are built in its pages.

1923

Scientific Advertising

Claude Hopkins publishes the rules of direct response: coupons, split tests, and copy measured by sales. It's the ancestor of everything you do with ads today.

Broadcast era
1930s

Radio and the soap opera

Procter & Gamble sponsors serial dramas to sell soap. Brands are producing the content, a century before branded content had a name.

1950s

Network TV

Three networks, one mass audience. The 30-second spot and the giant agency are the peak of top-down media.

1980

Cable fragments the audience

CNN, MTV, ESPN. Channels built for niches, which is the first sign that audiences would keep splitting.

Internet era
1994

First banner ad

An AT&T ad on HotWired. Ads become clickable and measurable.

2000

Google AdWords

Self-serve auctions and pay-per-click. Any business can buy attention by the click. Search becomes the biggest ad business in history.

2005–07

YouTube, the News Feed, iPhone

Anyone can publish video, feeds replace front pages, and media moves into everyone's pocket.

Algorithm & creator era
2016–18

Interest graph beats social graph

Instagram goes algorithmic and TikTok goes global. What you see depends on what holds your attention, not on who you follow. One unknown creator can now reach millions overnight.

2020s

Creators become the media companies

Trust moves from brands and outlets to people. Social commerce (TikTok Shop) puts the store inside the feed.

2025–26

Creator platforms pass TV, and AI makes content almost free

YouTube is the #1 thing on American TV screens. Generative AI drops the cost of making content to near zero, so the scarce thing is no longer the content. It's taste, trust and distribution.

3 · Fundamentals

How the machine works

Every media business, from a 1890s magazine to a TikTok creator, is the same five-step loop. What changes is who owns each step.

1MakeContent that people want. Once expensive, now nearly free.
2DistributeGet it in front of people. Owned by printers, then networks, now algorithms.
3Capture attentionThe scarce resource. Hooks, formats, habits.
4Earn trustRepeated attention turns into belief. This is why creators sell.
5MonetizeAds, subscriptions, commerce or licensing.
Law 1

Two customers

The audience pays with time; the advertiser pays with money. When the product is free, the audience is what's being sold.

Law 2

Distribution beats content

Whoever controls access to the audience takes most of the profit. Today that's Google, Meta, TikTok and YouTube. Ben Thompson calls this "aggregation theory".

Law 3

Hits rule

Returns follow a power law: a few pieces make most of the money. You win by making many bets cheaply and doubling down on winners, which is exactly what a machine is good at.

Law 4

Abundance moves the value

When something becomes cheap, value shifts to what is still scarce. AI makes content cheap, so the value moves to taste, trust, data and owned audiences.

Law 5

Rented vs owned audience

Followers on a platform are rented, and the landlord can change the rules overnight. Email lists, SMS and customers are owned.

Law 6

Formats travel, brands don't

A proven structure (a hook, a beat order, a proof moment) works across categories. That's why swipe, strip and inject works.

4 · Lessons for your machine

Content farms have been tried. Here's what killed them.

2009–2011

Demand Media

Pumped out millions of cheap articles written to match search terms, and went public at $1.5B. Google's 2011 "Panda" update demoted low-quality pages and the business collapsed. It was built on one algorithm and had no quality floor.

2012–2023

BuzzFeed

Mastered Facebook distribution and tested headlines at huge volume. When Facebook cut publisher reach, the traffic went with it, and BuzzFeed News shut down in 2023. It rented its audience.

The survivors

Who lasted

The winners joined volume with a quality bar, owned audiences (email, apps), and commerce revenue that didn't depend on one platform.

What this means for the build. (1) Every output passes a quality judge before it ships, so volume never turns into slop. (2) Spread across Meta, TikTok, YouTube and email so no single algorithm change can sink you. (3) Every ad should feed an owned list. (4) Swipe structures, never surface copy. Structure lasts, and copied surfaces get flagged.

6 · Learning materials

What to read, in order

Starred = read first. If you only read three, read the three stars.

History: how we got here

1
The Attention Merchants · Tim Wu (2016) ★ START HERE

The whole story, from the penny press to Instagram, told as one business: harvesting attention and reselling it. It covers sections 2 and 3 of this page in a single book.

2
Traffic · Ben Smith (2023) ★

The inside story of BuzzFeed and Gawker chasing viral reach. The closest history there is to what you're building.

3
The Master Switch · Tim Wu (2010)

Why every new medium starts open and ends up controlled by a few gatekeepers.

4
The Mirror Makers · Stephen Fox

The history of American advertising and the people who invented it.

Business: how the money works

5
Blockbusters · Anita Elberse

Why hits rule entertainment and why the winners make big bets.

6
Stratechery · Ben Thompson (newsletter)

Read his "Aggregation Theory" posts. They explain why platforms win and publishers lose.

7
1,000 True Fans · Kevin Kelly (essay, free)

Twenty minutes long. Explains the economics of owned audiences.

8
How Brands Grow · Byron Sharp

The evidence on how brands actually get bigger: reach new buyers and stay easy to notice.

9
Entertainment Industry Economics · Harold Vogel

The textbook. Use it to look things up, not to read cover to cover.

Craft: why an ad sells

10
Breakthrough Advertising · Eugene Schwartz ★

Awareness levels and market sophistication. The most useful framework for prompts that decide which angle to run.

11
Scientific Advertising · Claude Hopkins (1923)

Short. Every rule in it is still true.

12
Ogilvy on Advertising · David Ogilvy

Research-driven creative, with lots of pictures.

Why some things catch on: familiar and surprising at once.

Filterworld · Kyle Chayka

How algorithms flatten taste. It explains why everything in the feed starts to look the same.

Extremely Online · Taylor Lorenz

The history of creators and influencers, from blogs to TikTok.

7 · The path

Four weeks, about 30 minutes a day

Each week ends with one change to the machine, so what you learn ends up in a prompt.

Week 1 · History

The Attention Merchants

Output: one paragraph on "what business we're actually in", added to the brief template.

Week 2 · Craft

Breakthrough Advertising, ch. 1–3

Output: an awareness-level tag in the teardown prompt, so every swipe gets scored on it.

Week 3 · Money

Aggregation Theory + 1,000 True Fans

Output: a rule that every ad feeds an owned list, plus a platform-spread check.

Week 4 · Now

Traffic + the latest Gauge & IAB reports

Output: a quality-floor judge in the chain, the lesson from Demand Media and BuzzFeed.
