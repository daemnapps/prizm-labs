The first 90 frames

Hook design for short-form video and paid social. What a hook is made of, the seven ways it holds attention, and how to tell if your hook or your body is the thing that failed.

You're right that the hook is the gate: nothing downstream happens if the thumb doesn't stop. But a hook buys the next three seconds, not the sale. A hook that promises more than the body delivers spikes the 3-second number and then loses people, and the platform reads that drop-off as "bad video." The hook and the body have to be one thought.
Retention · how the platform sees your video

Three hooks, three curves

Illustrative shapes, not data from your account. They show the three failure patterns you'll see in Ads Manager or IG Insights.

A · clickbait

Great 3-second rate, cliff right after. The line opened a loop the body never closes. It attracts the curious, not the buyer, and cheap clicks that don't buy teach the algorithm to find more people who won't buy.

B · no stop

Most people are gone before the line is even said. The fix is in frame one, usually not the words. "Talking head at a desk" is a camera setup. It isn't a scroll stopper.

C · matched

The stopper, the line, and the body are one thought. The early drop is about the same as A, but the curve after it is shallow. This is the one that fills the funnel.

Funnel · which part of the video does which job

The hook owns one stage out of five

0–1s · frameStop

Scroll stopper. Something happening in frame one that looks odd with the sound off.

1–3s · lineOpen

The line names a problem in the viewer's own words and opens a loop.

3–15s · bodyHold

Pay the loop off a little at a time. Every beat earns the next one.

mid · proofBelieve

A demo, a result, a real person. This is where the hook's promise gets kept.

end · offerAct

CTA plus a landing page that continues the same thought. A mismatched page kills a good ad.

Mechanisms · why someone keeps watching

Seven ways a hook works

Viral hooks look different on the surface but almost always run on one of these. When you swipe a hook, name its mechanism first. That's the part you keep; the words are what you swap.

01 · Recognition

"That's literally me"

Names a private moment the viewer thought nobody had noticed. Your strongest mechanism for DR, because it filters for the buyer.

a behaviour + a time + an object, in their words

it describes a category instead of a moment

02 · Open loop

A question with a delayed answer

Starts something the brain wants finished. The answer must sit in the body, not the CTA.

result shown → how withheld

the payoff never comes (curve A)

03 · Pattern interrupt

Something where it shouldn't be

A visual that breaks the feed's rhythm: wrong object, wrong place, extreme macro, motion in a still feed.

ordinary scene + one wrong thing

it's strange but unrelated to the line

04 · Stakes

Something to lose

A cost, a mistake, or a risk the viewer might already be making.

the thing you do daily × what it's costing

it sounds like fear-mongering or breaks ad policy

05 · Contradiction

The opposite of what they believe

Flips a belief the viewer holds. Works best on problem-aware audiences who have tried the usual fixes.

the usual fix → why it made it worse

the flip is a stretch you can't prove

06 · Payoff first

Show the ending

Open on the after, the result, the reveal. Then rewind. The viewer stays to see how.

frame 1 = final frame

the result is so plain there's nothing left to learn

07 · Joke

A gap between what's said and what's true

Exaggeration, deadpan, or a mock-serious warning. The joke holds attention; the body supplies the real claim.

straight face + impossible statement

the joke is replaced with a literal benefit

Iteration · how to make five hooks from one that worked

Lock the format, change the fills

When a hook works, the reason is usually its sentence structure. Keep the fixed words and change only the blanks. Every fill should come from something a real customer said. This is the v18 format lock in your teardown machine, in one picture:

I'm[AGE]and I just found out[HABIT]was the reason for[PROBLEM]
V0The source's own line, brand words swapped. Your control; never improve it.
V1I'm 34 and I just found out my 6am pre-workout was the reason for the 3pm crash
V2I'm 29 and I just found out washing my face twice a day was the reason for the breakouts
V3I'm 41 and I just found out the dryer sheet was the reason for the itch

Example fills are made up to show the shape. In a real run each one cites a row from the brand's language bank.

Awareness · where the hook is allowed to start

Enter on the viewer's rung

A hook that names the product to someone who doesn't know they have the problem reads fine and is invisible to them. The colder the audience, the more the hook has to be about their life and not your product.

UnawareA moment, a scene, a story. A portrait of their life.Never: product, price, claim, the problem's name
Problem-awareThe problem in their words, plus a moment.Never: product or price
Solution-awareWhy the fixes they've tried failed. Contradiction works here.Never: price first
Product-awareProof, a demo, an objection handled.Avoid: re-explaining the problem
Most awareThe offer, the deal, the deadline.Avoid: long setup

Ship check for any hook

  • Frame one is something happening, and still reads as odd with the sound off.
  • The line, the text card and the frame are about the same thing.
  • The problem is named in the customer's words, and you can cite where you heard them.
  • The loop it opens gets paid off in the body, before the CTA.
  • It doesn't name anything the viewer's awareness rung can't hear yet.
  • It ships next to a control and four or five variations, and the numbers pick the winner.
