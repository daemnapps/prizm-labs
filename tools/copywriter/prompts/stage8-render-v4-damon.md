**The language above is queried, not dumped.** These rows were
chosen because of what they ARE — the kind of thing this stage needs — and
ranked by how loud they were and how well sourced. They are real sentences
real people said, each carrying who said it and where it came from.

Use their words. A row marked `ATTRIBUTION ASSUMED` is weaker evidence and a
row from a paid panel is not a customer speaking; weigh them accordingly and
never present either as something a customer said. If nothing in the rows
fits, say so — an empty result is a fact about the bank, not a licence to
write the sentence yourself.

**This is the stage that turns an argument into copy.** Everything upstream
worked out what to say and in what order. Nothing upstream decided what the
finished thing physically IS. That happens here.

**The source's medium is not the output's medium.** The source may have been a
video of text cards, a carousel, a still with a caption, or a block of copy.
Irrelevant. We are writing {output_formats} for {channel}, and the construct
is a set of persuasion mechanics that has to be re-expressed in that shape.

A card count does not become a line count. A visual reveal does not become a
line saying "wait for the end" — it becomes whatever *withholds and then pays
off* in running text. The construct's WHAT IS MEDIUM-BOUND section names each
of these and says what job it was doing; your task is to do that job by means
the output format actually has.

**Write each requested format in full, as finished copy.** Not a description
of the copy, not a plan for it, not beats to be written up later — the words,
exactly as they would be pasted into the platform.

For each format in {output_formats}:

- **Follow that format's row in the formats file.** A `caption` is not a short
  `long-story`; a `review` is not a `social-proof` in the first person. The
  row says what the thing is and what it reads as. Honour it.
- **Carry the same argument.** The moves, in the construct's order, doing the
  same jobs. If a move genuinely cannot survive in a format — a format too
  short to hold it — say which move you dropped and what that costs, rather
  than silently shipping a weaker argument.
- **Length comes from the FORMAT'S ROW, never from the source.** Every row
  states a word range. Hit it. A seven-second video with five text overlays
  does not produce a twelve-word ad — it produces a caption of caption length
  that uses that video's persuasion structure. Proportion transfers as
  SHAPE — where the turn falls, how much runs before the reversal — expressed
  across the format's own length. It is not a word count to copy.

- **Write sentences, not fragments.** On-screen text can be `At 68` alone
  because a face and a beach carry the rest. In a feed the words are alone and
  have to carry it themselves. **If what you produce could be mistaken for a
  shot list, a card stack, a set of overlays or a list of labels, it is not
  copy and it does not ship** — go back and write the actual sentences a
  stranger reads without the video.

  Before you hand anything over, read it as if the creative were not there.
  If it stops making sense without the picture, it was never copy.
- **Use the hook written for this format.** The hooks stage produced openings;
  use the control unless the format makes it impossible, and say so if it does.
- **Obey the voice construct absolutely.** If the lane is organic this is that
  person writing. The most common failure at this stage is that the argument
  survives and the voice does not — the copy becomes correct and anonymous.
- **Respect what the placement plan decided** about where the product enters
  and whether an offer appears at all. A format that carries no offer carries
  none.

**Formatting is part of the copy, not decoration.** Line breaks, where a
paragraph ends, whether there is a list, whether the first line stands alone —
these are how copy performs in a feed, and they belong in what you write. A
wall of text and the same words broken for the scroll are not the same ad.
Write it broken the way it should run.

**What you may not do:**

- **Never carry the source's units into the output.** No card numbers, no
  timestamps, no "0:04", no shot directions, no scene labels. If one appears,
  the medium leaked through and the copy is wrong.
- **An unfilled slot is searched, not inherited.** If the argument arrives
  carrying `[UNFILLED: …]`, go and look for it in the brand context supplied
  here before passing it through. Only a slot that is genuinely absent from
  every loaded file survives to the brief, and then it says where it was
  looked for.
- **ADAPT. DO NOT TRANSCRIBE.** The banks and the source give you what is
  TRUE — a real behaviour, a real objection, a real way of speaking. They do
  not give you the words to paste. A verbatim that no longer fits the frame
  gets rewritten to keep the truth and drop the frame. Lifting a customer
  sentence intact, or a source beat intact, because it is "on file" is the
  literal-mindedness this line exists to stop: the file is evidence, not
  a script.

- **A MONTH IN THE COPY MUST BE THE REAL ONE.** Today's date is at the top of
  this prompt — that is data, exactly like the avatar and the banks, and it is
  the only date this copy is allowed to be written from.

  The banks and the source hand you months because real people name real
  months. **Do not inherit theirs — substitute ours.** She wore a cardigan in
  the heat; the heat is now, so the month is the one it is now. A month
  carried across unchanged is the writer forgetting which day it is, and it
  reads as an ad written a season ago.

  A month earns its place when it does work — "a cardigan in August" lands
  harder than "a cardigan in summer" because it is specific and the reader is
  living in it. Use it when it does that. Drop it when it is decoration.

  Two things this does not touch: a genuine historical reference (a decade,
  an age, "when the kids were small") is a fixed point and stays as it is;
  and a named season must be the season this runs in and hold across the
  flight.

- **Never invent a claim to fill a format.** A long-form format does not
  entitle you to more claims than the files support; it entitles you to more
  room for the ones you have.
- **Never let a format become an excuse to add an ask** the placement plan
  ruled out.

**Every source gets every format, and more than one body per format.** A
buyer testing this needs options that differ in approach, not in wording. Two
bodies that say the same thing with different adjectives are one body.

Give me, per requested format:

**`FORMAT: <key>`**

**BODY A** — complete, broken as it should run, ready to paste.

**Count the words as you finish it, before you write anything else.** If it
is outside the row's range, rewrite it there and then and print ONLY the
rewritten version — never the long one with a note under it. You cannot go
back and edit what you have already written, so the version you print must
already be the final one. Then state its count.

The commonest failure is drafting to natural length and noticing afterwards.
Know the range before the first word: a caption is not a short essay, and
the room you have is the room in the row.

**BODY B** — a genuinely different run at the same argument: a different beat
promoted to the front, a different thing withheld, a different register. Same
structure underneath, same voice, same facts. Inside the range. Then its word
count.

Add a **BODY C** where the format's range is wide enough to hold a third that
is really different rather than padded.

**One line each on what that body is doing** — so a buyer can pick fast.

**What changed to fit this format** — moves compressed, combined or dropped,
and what each cost. If nothing was lost, say so.

Then, once, for the whole set:

**HEADLINES — exactly two, four to five words each.**

The short line beside the creative. Both must come **from this source** — its
own words, its turn, its punchline — never generic brand lines that would fit
any ad. If the source ends on a phrase that is already the headline, one of
the two is that phrase. Count the words and state the count after each.

**DESCRIPTIONS — exactly two, three words each.**

Three words. Not four. These sit under the headline and are the smallest,
hardest-working text in the ad: a flat statement of what it is or what it
does, no verbs of persuasion, no punctuation games. State the count after
each so the constraint is visibly met.

**Both headlines and both descriptions must survive the same rules as the
body:** the bound voice, no claim the files do not carry, nothing from the
source's own medium, and no result attached to a person the source cannot
identify.

## Write like the person, not like a brand about the person

The most common failure at this stage is not a wrong argument — it is copy
that is **correct and anonymous**. The teardown record carries a
`Fingerprint:` line and a VOICE FINGERPRINT section quoting how this person
actually builds a sentence. That is not colour; it is the specification.
Read it before writing a word, and obey it over any instinct about what
reads well.

Six moves separate copy that sounds like a person from copy that sounds like
a competent stranger. Every one of them is a cut, not an addition.

**1 · Noun first. Kill the narrator.** People say the thing; writers announce
that they are about to say the thing. Cut every *the first thing I noticed
was*, *what I found was*, *it's a…*, *there was a…*. Front the noun and the
sensation. Compare a narrated line — *"The first thing I noticed was the
texture. It's quite coarse and you can really feel the grains in it"* —
with the same fact said by a person: *"Coarse. You feel the grains."*

**2 · Contrast pairs beat explanations.** When a benefit needs a defence, the
defence weakens it. *"It left my skin smooth without feeling stripped, which
is what I want from a product like this"* explains. *"Smooth, not stripped"*
convicts. Find the two words the result sits between and drop everything
that justifies the preference.

**3 · Open on the harshest true thing they would say about themselves.**
This is a lookup, not a mood. Go to the language rows and find the
`self-descriptor` or `problem-language` entry that a brand would never dare
write — the phrase these people use about their own body, habit or history
when nobody is selling to them. Open on that. A tactful opening is a scroll;
the self-label is a stop, because the reader recognises themselves being
quoted rather than described. If the rows carry no such phrase, say so in
the checks — do not invent one, and do not settle for the polite version
while a harsher true one is sitting in the bank unused.

**4 · A limitation becomes an open loop, never a dead end.** Honesty is the
strongest asset an organic piece has, and it is usually spent badly:
*"I've only used it once so I can't say it works"* closes the door and ends
the ad. The same honesty aimed forward — *"whether it works, I can't say
yet; that takes consistent use, and I'll answer it then"* — makes the
limitation a reason to come back. Never claim the unproven; always leave the
question standing where someone can watch it get answered.

**5 · Cut what neither sells nor builds trust.** Pleasantries, mild invented
complaints, throat-clearing, anything that exists to sound balanced. A
manufactured small objection is a tell: real reviewers complain about what
actually annoyed them or say nothing.

**This outranks rule 6 when the two collide**, and they collide often. A
creator whose habit is the soft complaint-as-compliment — *"my only complaint
is I wish it came in a bigger size"* — has a habit that reads as a technique,
and a reader who has seen it twice discounts everything around it. Keep the
creator's structure, cut that beat. The habit is theirs; the credibility it
spends is ours.

**6 · Keep what is theirs.** The obligations and habits the fingerprint
records — a disclosure, a sign-off, a thank-you, a question to the audience —
stay, in their punctuation. A creator who ends on an exclamation does not
end on a full stop because the copy would look tidier.

**None of this applies to a brand-voice piece.** When the lane is not organic
and the speaker is the brand, obey the brand's own register instead — these
six exist to stop a PERSON being flattened into a brand, not to make brands
sound casual.

## Before you hand anything over

Run these six checks and **print the result of each**. They are mechanical,
they take one pass, and a check whose result is not written down did not
happen.

**Fix first, in the copy itself, then report.** A check that finds a problem
sends you back up to rewrite the affected line where it sits — the copy above
must already be the corrected copy by the time you write the receipt.
Describing a fix you did not apply is worse than not checking: it leaves the
wrong words in the place people copy from, and puts the right ones somewhere
nobody pastes from. If a body was over its range, the body above IS the
rewritten one, at the stated count. If a phrase came out, it is gone above,
not annotated below.

**1 · TIME.** Name every month, season and holiday in the copy, headlines and
descriptions included. Beside each, state the date at the top of this prompt
and confirm the two agree.

A month that is not the current one is a defect unless it is plainly
historical — go back, substitute the real month, and report it fixed. "The
bank said July" is not a defence: the bank recorded when she said it, not
when this runs.

**2 · FORM.** Confirm every body's count sits in its range — they should
already, because each was counted and fixed as it was written. This is a
tally, not a place to publish rewrites: if a body is still out of range here,
you printed the wrong version above and the copy is wrong. Then confirm each
reads as sentences a stranger could follow with the creative removed, and
that nothing could be mistaken for a shot list, card stack or overlays.

**3 · MEDIUM.** Confirm no unit from the source's own medium survived: no card
numbers, no timestamps, no shot or scene language, no on-screen-text framing.

**4 · SOURCE OF EVERY SPECIFIC.** For each concrete fact you wrote — an age, a
number, a duration, a price, a named behaviour, a quoted line — name the file
it came from **and what kind of language it is**. Anything you cannot trace is
either removed or rewritten into something you can. An unciteable specific is
an invented one.

Tag every one with exactly one of these:

- **`VERBATIM`** — a real person's own words, recorded as such. A quote from a
  customer, an interview, a ticket, a review. The file says so, or the entry
  carries a speaker and a source.
- **`AVATAR`** — a behaviour or belief the brand's own profile documents, in
  the profile's phrasing rather than a named person's. True about her; not a
  sentence anyone is recorded as having said.
- **`ANGLE`** — a line written by a marketer: a source card, an angle, a hook
  someone drafted. On-strategy and useful, but it is our language wearing her
  voice, not hers.
- **`FILE`** — a plain fact from the product or offer file: a price, a pack
  size, an ingredient, guarantee wording.

**Why this tagging exists.** These files deliberately record the difference —
one line will be marked *customer verbatim* and the line beside it marked
*source card* — and a stage that flattens them presents a copywriter's
invention as something a customer said. That is the exact difference between
copy built on evidence and copy built on our own assumptions dressed up as
evidence, and it is invisible in the finished text unless this check states
it.

Then give a one-line tally: how many of the specifics are `VERBATIM`, and
which lines in the copy are carrying `ANGLE` language. A piece resting mostly
on `ANGLE` is not disqualified — it is a piece whose realness has not been
demonstrated, and the person reading should be told that plainly rather than
having to check the banks themselves.

**THE OPENING CHECK.** Quote the language row your first line is built on,
with its tag. If it is not a `self-descriptor` or `problem-language` row,
name the harsher row you passed over and why — "there wasn't one" is a valid
answer only if the rows genuinely carry none.

**THE VOICE CHECK.** Quote the `Fingerprint:` line from the teardown record.
Then, for each body, print its first sentence and answer three things in a
few words each: does it front the noun or narrate · does its sentence length
and fragment habit match the fingerprint · does it end the way this person
ends things. Any "no" is a rewrite above, not a note here.

Then name the ONE line in the finished copy that could only have been written
about this source by someone who watched it — a phrase, an aside, a piece of
punctuation that is unmistakably theirs. If you cannot find one, the copy is
anonymous and you have not finished: go back and put the person into it.

Print these as a short block at the end, under `CHECKS`. Keep it terse — this
is a receipt, not an essay.
