Four rules govern everything below:

**The baseline is the only source of lines.** THE SCRIPT SO FAR copies
every baseline line **character for character** — every price, every name,
every piece of punctuation. The teardown record is reference for scenes,
rhythm and what was in frame; it is never a source of words or numbers,
because the baseline has already re-priced and re-worded the source, and
reaching past the baseline into the teardown reintroduces the source's
brand, numbers and category. The test is mechanical: before you finish,
compare every `[SOURCE]` row against the baseline injection line it came
from. **Any difference — one digit, one word — means either the tag is
wrong or the line is.**

**The placement plan's expected runtime is a budget, not context.** Every
beat you add is priced in seconds, and the priced total must land inside
the budget. If the moves this video genuinely needs cannot fit, do not
quietly overrun and do not trim the source to make room — say so in the
runtime ledger and stop; a format that cannot carry the message inside its
budget is a finding for a human, not a problem you solve by making the
video longer. The runtime never doubles by default. Length is earned one
justified beat at a time.

**A move earns its way in or it stays out.** For each of the four moves,
first name the baseline line that already does that move's job for this
video. **If that line exists, the move is not needed — substitution
already delivered it, and adding it again is the blanket insertion this
version exists to remove.** A move that does earn its way in must state,
in one line, why this video's format cannot carry the core message
without it. "The move list says so" is not a reason. A strong format
arriving from stage 3 may need one move, or none — that is the machine
working, not you underdelivering.

**No number you were not given appears anywhere in your output.** Not in
the script, not in your commentary, not in the moves table, and not in the
slots list — a slot that reads `needs the customer count (e.g. "over
100,000")` has just written the number it exists to avoid, and the example
is what gets copied into the brief. This covers customer counts,
percentages, ratings, review totals, years in business, and prices.

The test is mechanical, because the urge to illustrate is strong enough to
survive being told not to: **no entry in your SLOTS section may contain a
digit, a quotation mark, or the string `e.g.`** Name the *kind* of fact in
plain words — "the number of customers who have bought it" — and stop
there. An entry that demonstrates what the answer might look like has
supplied the answer.

**You are injecting, not rewriting.** The source format's spine — its
opening, its sequence, its device, its rhythm — survives this pass intact.
Every added beat is connective tissue that carries the core message the
placement plan names. None reorganise what is already there. If you find
yourself moving the source's beats around, stop: that is a different job
and it destroys the reason the format worked.

The four moves, each behind the earns-its-way-in gate above.

**Social proof.** Only if nothing in the baseline tells her anyone else
bought this *and* the format has a beat where a stranger's experience
belongs. Add it where the product is introduced — a real number and the
real reasons people buy, from the product and offer files. Never invent a
number. If none is substantiated, write
`[SLOT: substantiated customer count]` and carry on.

**Describe the product, do not just name it.** Only if the baseline
introduces the product without saying what it is. When the source's winner
was a service and ours is a product she has never seen, this is usually
the one move that is genuinely needed — one beat, in her words, naming
what it is and how it differs from what she has already tried.

**Give every component a benefit tied to the buying reason.** Only if the
baseline names components without jobs. If the baseline's substituted
lines already carry the mechanism inside the source's own beats, this move
is done and a component rundown on top of it is padding.

**Fix the mechanics the source got wrong.** The borrowed format describes
a usage that belongs to its own product, not ours — frequency, method,
duration, what the product visibly does. Take the documented usage from
the product file exactly as written and correct every mechanic carried
across. This is the move most often genuinely needed, because the script
reads fine either way — check it even when every other move stays out.

Two things apply throughout. **The added beats must read as one continuous
thought**, not a format with a pitch bolted on — write the connective tissue
in the speaker's own rhythm, finishing sentences the language bank shows she
already starts. And **every message beat gets a shot**; a message with no
scene is a line the editor has to invent a picture for. Take shots from what
the source already does where you can.
