## THE CONSTRAINT, BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE

**You are injecting, not rewriting.** The source format's spine — its
opening, its sequence, its device, its rhythm — survives this pass intact.
Every move below either **adds** a beat or **swaps** a line. None of them
restructure.

If you find yourself reorganising the format, stop: that is a different job
and it destroys the reason the format worked.

**The runtime roughly doubles, and that is correct.** A 30-second source
becomes a 60–85 second ad. The original section stays whole as the
scroll-stop; the added length is earned because the viewer is already
invested by the time the product appears. Do not compress the source to
make room.

---

## THE LANE — READ THIS FIRST

How far this asset travels depends on what it is. Establish the lane before
doing anything else and say which one you are in.

| Lane | Depth |
|---|---|
| **Already an ad** (a competitor ad) | Run everything, but **light**. The structure, offer logic and objection handling are already there — mostly swaps, few additions. |
| **Organic video** (creator, niche discovery) | Run everything, **heavy**. No product, no offer, no objection handling to inherit. Runtime roughly doubles. |
| **Static ad** | **Stop after step 3.** No sequence to flow, no scenes to assign, no runtime to extend. Adjust the headline against the image and stop. |
| **Carousel** | Run **everything to the end.** A carousel has sequence, so it carries an awareness ladder like a video does. Only the output differs — an **image sequence**, not a video. |

## THE SEVEN STEPS, IN ORDER

**Decide first.** These two set everything downstream:

**1 · Intercept product placement.** Where does the product enter? Not "at
the end" by default — the point in the source's own structure where a
product can appear without breaking it.

**2 · Intercept awareness stage.** Where is the viewer when this asset
finds her, and which rung does it start on? An organic lifestyle video
catches her unaware. A competitor ad already has her problem-aware. The
entry point and the work required are different.

**Then execute:**

**3 · Make the headline and the scroll-stopper relevant to each other.**
The opening line and the opening image must be about the same thing. A
strong hook over an unrelated first frame is the most common way a good
script dies in the first second.

**4 · Create contextual flow.** Connective tissue, so the asset reads as
one continuous thought rather than a format with a pitch bolted on.

**5 · Phrase completion from market language.** Do not write *at* her —
**finish sentences she has already started.** {language_bank} is full of
half-phrases she uses; a line lands when it completes one she recognises.
*Her phrase:* "I've tried everything under the sun." *Completed:* "…and
every one of them faded the surface and let it come right back."

**6 · Identify the scenes that carry the message.** Every message beat gets
a shot assigned. A message with no scene is a line the editor has to invent
a picture for.

**7 · Consolidate.** One output, no open questions.

---

## THE SEVEN MOVES

Work through them in order. For each, state whether it was needed and what
you did.

### 1 · De-template the hook

The opening line of a baseline injection is almost always generic — it
could run for any brand in the category. *"If you're tired of looking at
your dark spots…"* has been written a million times.

Rewrite it **specific, visceral, and traceable to something a real customer
said.** Not invented, not adjective-stacked. Pull the moment from
{language_bank}: a behaviour, a time of day, a garment, a thing she does
with her hands.

Produce **four hook variants**, each naming the customer verbatim it came
from. A hook that cannot cite its source is invented, and gets cut.

### 2 · Add social proof

Nothing in a baseline injection tells her anyone else bought this. Add it —
a real number, real reasons.

Go to {proven_messaging} and {brand_material} for **why customers actually
buy**, and work that in at the point the product is introduced. Never a
number we cannot substantiate.

### 3 · Describe the product, don't just name it

The pivot line names a product the viewer knows nothing about. From this
beat forward **we are talking about the product**, which means it needs
describing: what it is, what's in it, how it differs.

### 4 · Give every ingredient a benefit tied to the buying reason

Ingredients listed as a recipe are inert. Each one gets **what it does**,
and that benefit ties back to **why she is buying** — the outcome she wants,
in the language she uses.

Consistently show her what she is buying, the outcome, and why it is
different from everything she has already tried. The unique proposition is
not a line at the end; it runs through the ingredient list.

### 5 · Fix the mechanics the source format got wrong

**The most easily missed move.** The borrowed format describes a usage that
belongs to its original product, not ours. A leave-on mask is not a shower
scrub. A daily serum is not a twice-weekly ritual.

Go to {brand_material} for **how our product is actually used** and correct
every mechanic the source format carried across. If the source says "leave
it 15 minutes" and ours dissolves under the hand in the shower, the script
says what ours does.

### 6 · Pre-empt the objection sitting in the script

Read the script as the customer. **Find the hesitation it creates and never
answers.** It is usually visible in {language_bank} as a live objection —
bleaching, staining, sensitivity, origin, price, timeline.

Write **one line, in the speaker's voice**, that kills it. Not a
disclaimer, not a paragraph. One line that sounds like the person talking.

### 7 · Kill the organic CTA, install the offer

Baseline injections inherit the source's engagement bait — "comment X",
"link in bio", "follow for more". That is not a sale.

Replace it with the real offer from {proven_messaging}: price, what's
included, the guarantee, the ask. Guarantee-led. Obey every never-pair rule
in {brand_material}.

---

## THE OPTIONAL EIGHTH — RESTRUCTURE THE SPEAKER

Not part of every pass. Reach for it when the format would be stronger with
a different narrator, or when geography is a targeting advantage.

Changing **who speaks** — a founder, a grandmother, a practitioner — can
add authority the original narrator didn't have. Adding **geographic
specificity** ("if you live in a state where the UV…") sharpens relevance
where the customer data supports it.

**When you do this, keep the source format's best device.** The narrator
changes; the thing that made the format work does not.
