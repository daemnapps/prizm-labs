Here are the transformed scripts, one per source format: {stage4_outputs}
Here is the creator profile: {creator_profile}
Here is the product: {product_file}
Here is our offer: {offer_file}
Here is our customer language bank: {language_bank}
We are producing via: {production_route}
Videos owed: {video_count}

Turn these into the one document the maker receives. Everything upstream was
working material; this is the only thing that leaves the building.

Four rules govern everything below.

**Nothing unresolved crosses into the maker's document, and nothing is left
as a hole either.** If a script still carries a `[SLOT: …]`, work through these
in order:

1. **Fill it** if the fact exists anywhere in the supplied files. Most slots die
   here — they were placeholders for something a stage could not see but the
   brief can.
2. **Rewrite the beat** if the fact does not exist. Do not delete the line and
   move on: the beat was there because the script needed something at that
   moment, and a script with a beat missing plays worse than one that never had
   it. Work out what the line was *doing* — proof, contrast, reassurance,
   momentum — and write a new line that does the same job out of something the
   files can actually support. A proof beat that wanted a number it does not
   have can be carried by testimony that exists; a beat that wanted a date can
   be carried by what is honestly observable instead.
3. **Cut it only as a last resort**, when nothing in the files can do that job
   without a claim we cannot make.

**The one thing you never do is invent the missing fact.** Rewriting the beat
means finding a different true thing to say. It does not mean estimating the
number, softening it into a vague version of itself, or implying it without
stating it — "thousands of people", "loved by so many", "results in weeks" are
all the invented fact wearing a hat. If the replacement line needs a fact, that
fact comes from the files with the same rigour as any other.

None of this bookkeeping appears in the document. The maker gets the finished
lines and nothing about how they were arrived at — no note that a beat was
rewritten, no trace of what used to be there. The reasoning is yours to do and
theirs to never see.

**Offer figures come from the offer file — fill them in.** Prices, pack sizes,
savings and the guarantee wording are in the supplied offer file; write them into
the brief as they stand there. Do not leave a slot for a figure the file already
gives you: a brief that says `[SLOT: price]` when the price is sitting in front
of it is the document failing at its one job.

The single exception is a promotion the file does not contain — a sale running
this week, a code, a deadline. Only then write `[SLOT: current_promotion]`, list
it under fill-before-send, and a person fills it from the pricing sheet as the
brief goes out. If the file has no live promotion recorded, the brief simply
carries the evergreen price and no slot at all.

**Give the maker the words, and write them straight to them.** Every concept
carries the actual lines, in order, sitting with the shot they are spoken over.
Not beats to interpret, not points to convey, not a list of true things to work
in somewhere — the sentences themselves. Assume the maker has never built an ad
and should not have to invent any part of one: what to point the camera at, what
to say, and how to say it are all on the page.

**The whole document is written in the second person, to the maker.** "You say."
"You pick it up." Never "they say" or "the creator does" — the maker is the
reader, not the subject, and a document that talks about them in the third person
reads like a spec sheet they were copied on. Their own phrasing is welcome and
the note to them says so, but that is the maker changing a line that already
exists, never the maker filling a hole we left. Everything upstream — the
teardown, the spec, the injection, the passes — exists to produce exactly this,
and stopping short of the words throws that work away at the last step.

**Every concept names the source it came from and the habit it uses.** The
maker does not see the teardown, but they should be able to tell this was
built for them rather than sent to everyone. Name which of their own posts the
structure came from, and which of their habits each concept leans on. This is
also what stops one brief going out repeatedly with the names changed.

**Every image in this document must be looked at before it ships.** A brief
carries reference frames so the maker can see the shot instead of interpreting
a paragraph — which means an unreviewed image is worse than no image at all. It
goes out under our name, to a real person, and whatever is in it is what we
told them to make.

The check is not optional and it is not a glance at the prompt that produced
the frame. **Every frame is opened and described by a vision model before it
enters the brief**, and anything that comes back wrong is deleted, not
captioned around. A frame fails and is cut if any of these are true:

- **Anyone in it is unclothed, partly unclothed, or ambiguous about it.** This
  is the failure that has actually happened: a prompt that left clothing
  unstated, in a setting that invites undress, returned an undressed subject
  and reached a published brief. Any frame with a person is checked for this
  first, before anything else.
- It shows a person the brief has no likeness rights for.
- It shows the product with invented branding, or a competitor's.
- It contradicts its own scene — wrong room, wrong object in hand, a light
  burning when the scene says it was switched off.
- It shows a claim we do not make: a result the product has not been shown to
  deliver, a problem pictured as already gone, the subject or the product
  altered to look better than it really is.

The same check runs on any source video before its teardown is trusted. **A
brief with an unchecked image in it is not finished** — do not hand it over and
hope. The check happens whether or not anyone sees it happen.

**The maker must never have to ask a question.** If they would have to guess a
length, a shot, a product, or what they are allowed to say, the brief has
failed and the fix is in the brief, not in them.

Give me the brief.

What the maker receives. Write it to them, warmly, in plain language. No
jargon from this process appears anywhere in it — no stages, no lanes, no
awareness rungs, no move numbers.

Open with a short note to them by name: how many videos, that each one is built
from a post of their own, and that everything they need is on the page — the words
are written out, and they are free to say them in their own way.

Then the requirements every video shares: format and orientation, length
range, file delivery, music and rights constraints, anything the platform or
the agreement demands.

Then the brand direction that applies across all of them, and the offer
direction: what may be said about the offer, the exact guarantee wording, and
when to use it. Where a close needs a figure that moves, leave
`[SLOT: current_promotion]` standing rather than filling it from the offer file.
Keep it short — this is context, not instruction.

Then one section per concept, up to the number of videos owed. Each carries:

- **A name** for the concept, so it can be referred to in one word

- **The inspiration, and what we did to it** — the post this was built from,
  **with a link they can open**, and then the engineering laid out so it is
  obvious rather than mysterious. Three parts, in this order:

  1. **What that video does** — its actual beats, in order, as the teardown
     recorded them. Not a vibe; the structure. They made it, so they will
     recognise it instantly and trust the rest of the document more for it.
  2. **Why it works** — the one or two mechanics that carry it, named plainly.
  3. **What we changed to make it ours** — beat by beat where it matters: this
     beat stays exactly as it was, this one now carries the product, this one
     was cut and here is why. A creator who can see the substitution can
     protect it when something goes wrong on the day; one who cannot will
     improvise over the part that mattered.

  If no permalink is in the supplied files, write their profile link plus the
  post's date and caption so they can find it in seconds — never a bare slot,
  and never a caption alone.

- **The core idea** — two or three sentences on what the video is doing and how
  it lands its point. Written to the maker, not about them.

- **The scenes** — numbered, in order, and the body of the whole document. Each
  scene carries three things and nothing else:

  1. **On screen** — what is in frame, in one line, addressed to the maker.
  2. **You say** — the words, written out exactly as they should be said. If a
     scene has no speech, write *nothing spoken* and give what carries it
     instead: a text card, with its words, or the action.
  3. **How** — the delivery in a few words. To the lens. Low and close.
     Unhurried. Over your shoulder.

  Every true thing about the product belongs inside a line you give them to say,
  in the scene where they say it. Do not collect the true things anywhere else in
  the document — a fact they have to place themselves is a fact they will place
  wrong or skip. The claim rules bind every one of those lines.

- **Must-have shots** — a plain list, one shot per line, of what has to be in
  frame for the video to work at all. Shots only, no reasoning.

- **A reference frame for every scene, taken from their own video first.** The
  brief carries a picture on each scene so the maker can see the shot instead of
  reading it. Where that picture comes from is not a free choice:

  1. **Their own video is the first source, always.** The source they are
     replicating was already downloaded and torn down frame by frame — their face,
     their room, their light, their hands, their real clothes are all in it. For every
     scene, name the **timestamp in their video** whose framing matches, and take
     the frame from there. A still of them actually doing it beats anything
     generated, and it cannot invent a person, a garment, or a product that does
     not exist.
  2. **Generate only what their video does not contain** — a product beat their reel
     never had, an angle they never showed. When you do, the generation takes
     **their extracted frame as its likeness reference** so it is them, not a
     stranger who is roughly their age. Say in the prompt which timestamp the
     reference frame came from.
  3. **Never a generic stand-in.** A frame showing someone who is not them,
     wearing clothes they do not own, is worse than no frame: they will shoot
     what they see. If neither route is available for a scene, leave it without
     a picture and say so.

  Every generated prompt still states, in full: framing and light; **what the
  person is wearing, completely** — never left to inference, because a setting
  that invites undress, with clothing left unstated, will produce an undressed
  subject otherwise, which has actually happened and reached a published brief;
  **the product exactly as it really looks**, colour and finish from the product
  file and never assumed from its name; and the exclusions — no on-screen text,
  no invented branding, no result the product has not been shown to deliver, no
  retouching that makes the subject look better than it really is.

  If a scene's shot cannot be described without breaking a claim rule, leave that
  scene without a picture rather than writing a prompt that breaks it.

  **How the frames are actually made** (settled 2026-08-19, after a run that
  worked): the still from their video goes to an image model as the INPUT image,
  image-to-image, and the prompt changes only what the scene needs. Seeding from
  their own frame is what holds their face, their room and their light steady across
  twelve scenes — text-to-image alone produced a different person every time, and
  once produced an undressed one.


- **Length**

Close with anything they need to know that is neither brand nor concept —
who to send questions to, what happens next.

## THE SCENE BLOCK — a fixed shape, not a style choice

Added at v9. Stage 6 puts a reference picture on every scene by cutting a still
out of the creator's own reel. It can only do that if the scene says **where in
her reel to cut**. Up to v8 the scene format was left to you, three runs
produced three different shapes, and stage 6 could not read any of them — so it
never produced a single frame. This section is the contract between the two
stages. It is not a suggestion about formatting.

Every scene, in every concept, is written **exactly** like this:

```
**Scene 3 · 0:18–0:24**

**On screen** — what the camera sees, in one paragraph: framing, what she is
doing, what is in her hands, the light, and — always — what she is wearing.

**She says** —
> the spoken line, verbatim

**How** — the delivery note.

*Reference: her reel at 0:41 — same framing, nothing new to look at.*
```

Four rules govern that block:

1. **`**Scene N · <start>–<end>**` is the opening line of every scene.** Bold,
   the word Scene, the number, a middle dot, then the scene's own span in the
   finished video. Not a heading, not a dash, not a table row.
2. **Every scene ends with a `*Reference:*` line naming a timestamp in her own
   reel** — the moment that shows this shot, or the nearest thing to it. If the
   shot exists in her reel, add *same framing, nothing new to look at*; stage 6
   will then ship her real photograph instead of generating anything, which is
   always the better frame. If the shot does not exist in her reel, still name
   the closest timestamp: it is the seed that keeps her face, her room and her
   light in the generated frame.
3. **The On screen paragraph states what she is wearing, in full, every time.**
   Never "as before", never left out. A generated frame inherits whatever the
   seed still happens to show, and on 2026-08-20 seven frames came back as an
   identifiable real person in swimwear because a scene left clothing unstated.
   A scene that does not state clothing gets no picture at all.
4. **A concept starts with a `# ` heading.** Scene numbering restarts at 1 in
   each concept; the heading is what keeps three videos from collapsing into one.

If a brief is written to no one — swipe research with no creator attached —
there is no reel to reference. Write the scene blocks the same way, and put
`*Reference: none — no creator footage for this brief.*` on every scene. Stage 6
is not run on those briefs at all.
