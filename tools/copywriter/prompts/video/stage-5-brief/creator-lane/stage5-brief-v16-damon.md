## Naming and describing the concept

**The `# ` heading names the concept.** Short, the way you'd refer to it in
conversation — not a slogan, not a headline.

**Under the heading, one to three sentences describing what this concept is** —
the core idea it runs on, in plain language. Pull this from the concept's own
material (the placement plan, the injection, the stage-4 passes); never invent
a rationale that isn't already there. This replaces any explanation the maker
would otherwise have to guess at from the scenes alone — it's the only place in
the document where the "why" appears, and it stays to one paragraph.

## The opening section — the part that gets mangled

Every hook in the hook set goes in, control first, then each variation under
its own name. For each one, two labeled lines — never blended into one
paragraph, never left for the maker to infer:

- **`Film:`** the scroll stopper — the frame itself, exactly as the hook set
  describes it.
- **`Say:`** the spoken line, word for word. If the format has no speech,
  label it **`Card:`** instead and give the on-screen text.

A maker reading this must never have to guess whether a line is something she
says out loud or just a description of the scene. `Say:` means it comes out
of her mouth on camera. `Film:` never contains a spoken line.

**The scroll stoppers are already written. You are copying them, not writing
them.** The hook set hands you each one as a described frame. Reproduce that
frame — the angle, the distance, where the subject is, what is in shot, the light.

This is the single thing that goes wrong most. On one run the source was a
woman lying on a towel on a beach in midday sun, the hook set said exactly
that, and the brief opened on a bathroom counter in the morning instead. A
frame was then generated of the bathroom, and the entire reason the source
stopped anyone scrolling was gone. **The scroll stopper is the swipe.** It is
the most valuable thing in the teardown and the least replaceable.

So:

- **Never relocate the opening.** If the source's first frame is outdoors, the
  opening is outdoors. Do not move it somewhere easier to shoot.
- **Never substitute a product shot for a scroll stopper.** A tube held up to
  camera is not an attention device; it is what the source spent its opening
  earning the right to show.
- **Never paraphrase it into a summary.** "Relaxing outside" is not the
  frame. The angle, the distance and what fills the frame are the frame.
- **Scene 1 of the shot list IS the control's scroll stopper**, written out as
  a scene with its wardrobe line. The two must describe the same picture. If
  Scene 1 and the control disagree, Scene 1 is wrong.

Check the teardown record if a scroll stopper is unclear — it holds the source
shot by shot, and the opening frame is in its first row. All hooks get filmed;
nobody picks.

## Every scene

Four labeled lines, always in this order, never merged into one paragraph:

1. **`**Scene N · start–end**`** — bold, the word Scene, number, middle dot, span.
2. **`Film:`** what the maker does, in plain sentences — where they are, the
   light, what is in their hands, what they do with it. This is direction for
   the camera. **It never contains a line she says out loud** — a spoken line
   always goes under `Say:`, never folded into the filming direction.
3. **`Wearing:`** on every single scene, spelled out in full — garment,
   colour, sleeves, hair, jewellery. Never "as before", never "same as scene 2",
   never left out. A scene with no wardrobe line is a broken scene: the picture
   for it gets generated from nothing and comes back as a stranger in clothes
   the subject does not own.
4. **On its own line, as a markdown quote (`> `):** either **`> **Say:**`**
   followed by the exact words she speaks on camera, in quotes — the
   voiceover, nothing else — or, if nothing is spoken in this scene,
   **`> **On-screen text:**`** followed by the card copy instead. Keep the
   leading `> ` on this line always; it is what keeps the spoken line out of
   the picture the scene generates. A scene never has both — it either has
   someone talking or it has a card, never both. Never leave the maker to
   guess which one a line is — a quote mark alone is not a label.
5. **`![Scene N](—)`** on its own line.
