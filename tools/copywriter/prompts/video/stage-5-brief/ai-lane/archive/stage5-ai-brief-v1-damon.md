**Nobody is filmed.** This route casts no creator and points no phone at
anyone. Every person, place and shot in this document gets generated: a cast
station makes each character once, a voice station speaks the lines, a scenes
station composes one still per frame, a motion station turns stills into
clips. This brief is the only thing those stations read, so everything they
need must be on the page as generatable text. **A detail this document does
not carry does not exist downstream** — there is no set to glance at and no
person to ask.

That changes the grammar, not the job. The job is still the swipe: the
source's structure, its shots, its script, reproduced for our brand. What
changes is that the person, the room and the camera — everything a creator
brief leaves to whoever shows up with a phone — are decisions this document
makes, in writing, once.

# THE FOUR LOCKED BLOCKS

Written once per concept, before any frame. The stations paste these into
every generation job, so they are the consistency of the whole piece: a
frame never restates what a block holds, and a block never leaves a gap a
frame would have to improvise around.

## THE CAST — who exists

One block per person on screen. **The character is built from the teardown
record's observed subject**: the same age range, the same presence, the same
wardrobe — an original person in their mold. Never the source creator
themself, and never anyone recognisable: this document must not direct the
generation of any real person's likeness, the swiped creator's included.

Each block, in concrete physical detail, in this order: age, skin tone and
texture, face, hair — colour, length, how it is worn — and build. Always
natural skin with visible pores, no digital smoothing, no beauty-filter
look, natural anatomy. Name the character in caps — MAYA, THE FRIEND — and
that name is how every frame refers to them from then on.

**Load-bearing body details.** Before writing the cast, read your own shot
list. Any part of the body a frame depends on gets named in the block: the
forearms in a before-and-after demo, the hands if there is a close-up, the
hairline if hair is the subject, the smile if a beat lands on it. Write what
state it is in and where it appears. A detail the block does not carry gets
regenerated differently in every scene — and a demo shot on inconsistent
forearms is not a demo.

**Wardrobe lives in the cast block.** Every outfit the source has, garment
by garment — garment, colour, sleeves, hair, jewellery — named exactly, and
only those outfits. The teardown record lists the wardrobe; use it and
nothing else.

Anyone else on screen gets their own smaller block, named by role — THE
FRIEND, THE PERSON WHO SENT IT — with enough identity to generate them
consistently across their frames, and no more.

## THE WORLD — where it happens

One named block per distinct location in the source — THE KITCHEN, THE CAR,
THE BEACH. Each is the place written so it can be generated from the text
alone: the surfaces, the furniture, where the window is and what the light
is doing, the believable clutter, what sits within reach. Outdoors works the
same way: the sand, the towel, the hard midday sun.

**The locations are the source's.** Same places, described sharper — never
relocated somewhere easier, never added to. If the source cuts to a shop,
there is a shop block. The teardown record holds what each place looked
like; the block is that place in generatable words.

## THE CAMERA — the realness dial

One paragraph that decides whether this looks like a phone or a film, and it
comes **from the teardown record's own camera character**, not from taste.
If the source is self-shot: propped phone, slight wide-angle distortion,
amateur framing a touch off-centre, true-to-life colour, mild sensor grain,
real skin texture — and the negatives spelled out: no cinematic grading, no
studio light, no beauty retouching, no editorial gloss. If the source is
polished, write the polished block instead. One camera per concept; frames
never argue with it. Making a self-shot format look like a film is the same
mistake as relocating its opening — it deletes the reason the source worked.

## THE PRODUCT — real packshot, never described

The product's appearance is never generated from words. The block names the
product — its exact name from the product file — and states the requirement:
**every generation job with the product in frame carries the real packshot
as a reference image, and the render must match it, label crisp and
legible.** The brief never describes the label, the colours or the shape
from imagination; if no packshot exists at generation time, that is a stop
for the owner, not a licence to describe one.

# WHAT TO GENERATE

## EVERY OPENING GETS A PICTURE

Openings are written as numbered units — `**Opening 1 · Control**` — in the
same shape the frames use, each followed by its own image placeholder. This
is not cosmetic: the picture stage finds what to illustrate by matching that
shape, so an opening written any other way silently gets no picture.

Every hook in the hook set goes in, control first, then each variation under
its own name. For each one, two labeled lines:

- **`Shot:`** the scroll stopper — the frame itself, exactly as the hook set
  describes it, in third person: the character by name, the place by its
  world block, the action.
- **`Say:`** the spoken line, word for word. If the format has no speech,
  label it **`Card:`** instead and give the on-screen text.

**The scroll stoppers are already written. You are copying them, not writing
them.** Reproduce each described frame — the angle, the distance, where the
subject is, what is in shot, the light. Never relocate the opening, never
substitute a product shot for a scroll stopper, never paraphrase it into a
summary. The scroll stopper is the swipe: it is the most valuable thing in
the teardown and the least replaceable. Frame 1 of the shot list IS the
control's scroll stopper, written out as a frame; if they disagree, Frame 1
is wrong. All hooks get generated; nobody picks.

## THE UNIT IS THE FRAME — AND THE SCRIPT DECIDES HOW MANY

**Every frame exists because a line or a beat in the script calls for it.
One shot, one frame. No frame without something to carry.**

A source that is already a finished ad is a format to replicate: its shots
are the plan, transcribed from the teardown's Visuals column, split where a
row holds several shots — and that count is your count. A source that is an
organic post is a hook, not an ad: the close pass hands you the full script
and **your shot list covers all of it, to the last line** — where the
source's shots run out, the script is still talking, so there are still
frames. Before writing, count what the script needs — every spoken line,
every card, every held beat — and that is your frame count. A shot list that
stops before the script does is unfinished; a frame with nothing to carry is
invented. Both are the same mistake: the count coming from somewhere other
than the script.

If the source cuts to a shop, you have a shop. If it goes back to the keyed
talking head for one line, that is a frame. A shot that is awkward to
generate is still a shot — describe it and let the owner decide. Never
silently drop one; a dropped shot is as wrong as an invented one, and it is
the reason a swiped format stops working.

## Every frame

Eight labeled lines, always in this order, never merged into one paragraph:

1. **`**Frame N · start–end**`** — bold, the word Frame, number, middle dot,
   span.
2. **`Source:`** the source shot this frame replaces, quoted from the
   teardown record's Visuals column, shortened to its first few words.
   **Frame N replaces source shot N — the order never changes.** If you
   cannot write this line, the frame has no source shot and should not
   exist — unless it is script-backed past where an organic source ends, in
   which case write `Source: none — script-backed` and nothing else.
3. **`Shot:`** the picture, in the third person, at most two sentences of
   action. Name who (the cast name), where (the world block's name plus the
   one detail that anchors it — the window light, the sand), and the single
   action, described so a generator can produce it: the tug, the pinch, the
   hand stopping. If the frame is built — keyed, picture-in-picture, split
   screen, a screen recording — **say the construction first**, then the
   content: what she occupies, what plays behind her, which corner, how the
   frame divides. A keyed opener reproduced as a plain shot throws away the
   thing that made it work.

   **Third person, always.** MAYA lifts the tube; she turns to the window.
   Never "you" — there is no you. This document directs a generator, not a
   maker, and a "you" in a shot line is a person the machine will put in the
   picture.
4. **`Hear:`** what the clip's audio is. A talking frame: `her line,
   lip-synced — nothing else`. A silent or b-roll frame: the ambience in
   words, taken from what is physically in the frame — the tap running, the
   box being opened, room tone — **with the negatives spelled out**: no
   music, no dialogue. Never atmosphere you imagine; if nothing in the frame
   makes a sound worth naming, write `room tone only — no music, no
   dialogue` and stop.
5. **`Wearing:`** on every single frame, spelled out in full from the cast
   block's outfit, word for word — garment, colour, sleeves, hair,
   jewellery. Never "as before", never "see cast". The same outfit repeats
   exactly until the source itself changes clothes, and a change of clothes
   is a change of time or place that must be visible in the story. Outfits
   drifting frame to frame is the clearest sign a brief was invented rather
   than observed.
6. **`On screen:`** the words burned into the picture at this moment, copied
   **verbatim** from the teardown record's On-Screen Text column, in quotes.
   It lags, it shortens, it stops mid-sentence — copy what the source put on
   screen, never tidy it, never re-derive it from the spoken line. If the
   record shows nothing, write `On screen: nothing` — a real instruction. A
   frame can carry a caption and speech at the same time, and most do.
7. **On its own line, as a markdown quote (`> `):** **`> **Say:**`** followed
   by the exact words spoken in this frame, in quotes. This line is the
   voice take: it goes to the voice station word for word, and its
   punctuation is the pacing direction. If nobody speaks, write
   `> **Say:** nothing — picture only`. Keep the leading `> ` always; it is
   what keeps the spoken line out of the picture the frame generates.
8. **`![Frame N](—)`** on its own line.

## EVERY FRAME STANDS ALONE

Each frame becomes its own generation job, run by something that has not
read any other frame. So every Shot line names the character and the place,
every time, even when nothing has changed. "She stays exactly where she is"
is a reference with nothing behind it — the job that receives it will put
her anywhere. Write it as a first frame, every time: **"MAYA, lying on the
white towel on the sand, hands behind her head, nothing moving."** The
identity details live in the cast block and are never restated — the name
carries them — but the name, the place and the action are restated in full
on every frame.

Where the camera sits — propped on the counter, lens at chest height — goes
at the END of the Shot line, after the picture is described, never as the
opening words.

## THE SHOT LIST IS AUDITED AGAINST THE SOURCE BEFORE YOU FINISH

Last thing before you return anything. Walk the teardown record's rows in
order against your frames, and satisfy yourself of four things:

1. **Every shot in the record has a frame.** None dropped.
2. **Every frame is accounted for** — by a shot in the record, or by a line
   in the script that runs past where the source ends. A frame with neither
   behind it is invented; delete it.
3. **The script is covered to its last line.** If your final frame lands
   before the script does, the brief is unfinished.
4. **Each frame is doing what its source shot did** — same place, same
   action, same construction. Ours substitutes the brand, never the
   behaviour. If the source applies something, ours applies something; if
   the source is keyed, ours is keyed.

Then one check this lane adds: **read the cast and world blocks against the
finished frames.** Every body part a frame shows close is named in the cast
block; every place a frame uses has a world block; every outfit written on a
frame exists in the cast block. A frame that needs something the blocks do
not hold means the block is unfinished, not the frame.

If a source shot genuinely cannot be reproduced with our product — the
mechanic does not exist for us — say so in one line under that frame and
keep the frame. That is a finding for the owner, not something to quietly
paper over.

## THE CONVERGENCE CHECK

Before this pass ends, find the single most important beat in the concept —
the one the whole thing is built to deliver. Read its three lines together:
`Shot:`, `Hear:`, `Say:`. They must point at the same thing at the same
moment. If they diverge at that beat, fix the beat — not by inventing, but
by deciding which of the three is wrong and bringing it back to the other
two.

## Do not write a transcript at the end

The finished brief ends with every spoken line laid out in order. **That
section is assembled from your `Say:` lines automatically — you do not write
it.** Put each line where it belongs, on its frame, and the transcript takes
care of itself.

# THIS DOCUMENT IS SENDABLE AS IT STANDS

**Nothing unresolved reaches this document.** Not a `[SLOT: …]`, not a
bracket, not a note to whoever fills something in. It goes to the generation
bench and has to read as finished.

Upstream stages write `[SLOT: …]` wherever a fact was needed and the brand
files did not hold it. That is the anti-invention rule working, and you do
not undo it. What you do depends on which of two kinds the slot is:

**A slot asking who someone is — cast it.** On this route, who someone is
was never a fact to look up; it is a decision this brief makes. Write the
character's block from the teardown record's observed subject, the way THE
CAST says, and the slot is resolved. A frame is never held up waiting for a
name, and no real person is ever the answer.

**A slot asking for a brand fact — cut the line.** A price, a promotion, a
date, a guarantee wording: if it is not in one of the files above, the
sentence carrying it does not appear. Say nothing rather than something
unapproved. **Generalising a slot is filling it** — turning
`[SLOT: current promotion]` into "the bundle deal" has announced a promotion
nobody approved, in a document that generates a voice saying it out loud.

## Naming and describing the concept

**The `# ` heading names the concept.** Short, the way you'd refer to it in
conversation — not a slogan, not a headline. Under the heading, one to three
sentences describing what this concept is — the core idea it runs on, pulled
from the concept's own material, never a rationale invented here. It is the
only place in the document where the "why" appears.

# WHAT DOES NOT GO IN

- No source link, no reference lines or timestamps into anyone else's
  footage.
- No real person's name or likeness, anywhere — no identity anchors, no
  creator, no celebrity, nobody recognisable.
- No model names, no generation settings, no costs, no station mechanics.
  The brief says what to make; the machine decides how.
- No "Don't say" list, no compliance language, no guarantee wording.
- No explanation of why anything was chosen, changed, cut or kept, beyond
  the one paragraph under the concept name.
- No runtime totals, beat counts or budgets.
- No sign-off, no thanks, no lengthy introduction. Nothing follows the last
  frame.

# TONE

Short sentences. Every frame reads in three seconds flat: what the shot is,
what it sounds like, what she wears, what is on screen, what she says —
nothing to parse, nothing left to a set that does not exist.

# WHAT AMPLIFICATION MEANS HERE

You may make the same thing land harder. You may not make a different thing.

**Free — how it is delivered:**

- the rhythm and syntax of a spoken line
- how vividly a shot or a world block is described
- which physical detail gets named

**Locked — what is being said:**

- the beats, their order, and the structure they sit in
- every claim, number, price, offer, guarantee and timeline
- which product does what, and what is in it
- who is on camera — the observed subject's mold — and what they are wearing

The test: if a change alters what a viewer **learns**, it is out of scope.
If it only alters how hard that lands, it is the job.

Every specific in this prompt — an age, a gender, a garment, a body part, a

None of it describes the asset in front of you. Read an example for the

cast block is built from what the record observed, and any pronoun in an
example above is about that example, not about them.
