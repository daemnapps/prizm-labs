stage1-teardown-v11-damon.md

One video in, one objective record out — scenes, mechanics, psychology.

Here are the sections an argument is built from — the only labels a scene
may carry: {sections}

Take this individual video and break it down completely so I can
reverse-engineer it. Break down the scenes, the script, the characters, and
what the characters are doing and how they're doing it — each and every
element individually, timestamped, scene by scene. Go deep. A long,
heavily detailed output is what I want — do not compress or summarize.

You are building a reproduction spec: someone who has never seen this video
must be able to re-create it — the person, the clothes, the room, the shots,
the words — from your teardown alone. If a detail would matter to someone
re-creating the video, it belongs in your output.

Describe the subject in front of you, not a default. Every example in
this prompt is illustrative only — an age bracket, a garment, a style
reference — and none of them describe the video you are looking at. Take
gender, age, register and aesthetic from what is actually on screen. Never
carry over the framing of a previous teardown, and never reach for the
category's stock subject. A record that describes someone other than the
person in the video is worthless no matter how detailed it is.

Every video is scenes. A scene is a section of the asset — one of the
functional phases named in THE SCENE MECHANICS below. A scene has
frames: one frame per distinct visual change, same as the objective
record's rows have always been. Frame is the new name for that row;
nothing about when a new one starts has changed.

This is observation, not evaluation. You are recording what is
demonstrably there so it can be analysed later — not judging whether it is
good, not recommending anything, not writing about the brand. Record brand
names when they appear on products in frame, because that is a fact about
the video. Never editorialise about the brand, never assess the product, and
never describe the video as effective or ineffective outside the sections
that explicitly ask for mechanism. Abstraction happens downstream; your job
is an accurate record.

Three laws govern everything:

Accuracy law. Never guess. If you cannot see or hear something clearly,
write `[UNCLEAR at 0:12]` instead of a plausible guess. Mark anything you
inferred rather than directly observed with `(inferred)`. A confident wrong
detail is worse than a marked gap — wrong details get built into real
briefs.

Consistency law. Every element gets ONE name, used identically
everywhere it appears. Every detail cited anywhere in your output must
exist in the objective record. State the age range ONCE, in brackets
(e.g. `[50-55]`), and reuse that exact range everywhere.

Depth law. The record's detail demands must never thin the analysis.
Sections 2 and 3 go as deep as the record goes precise: every phase's
"Why it works" names the exact words, visual choice, or pacing doing the
work; every "What it sets up" names the specific next move it enables,
never a generic outcome. If length becomes a pressure, cut nothing —
write longer.

I need four things.

1. THE OBJECTIVE RECORD

Open with one line, labeled `Sound form:` — SPOKEN, SUNG, RAPPED,
MUSIC ONLY or SILENT, and say which for how much of the runtime. LISTEN
for this; it is not visible on screen. If the words are SUNG or RAPPED,
the piece is a song and its script is lyrics, not narration — say so
plainly in this line, because everything downstream is written from it.
Describing the delivery as "melodic" or "musical theater style" without
naming the form is the miss this line exists to prevent: a sung ad became
a spoken brief that way, and the format that made it worth swiping was
lost (2026-09-02).

Where the form is SUNG or RAPPED, also give: the structure (verse,
chorus, bridge — and which lines repeat), roughly where the hook line
lands, and whether the vocal is one voice or several.

Then a Character & Setting profile:

- Who appears — age range in brackets, physical description, hair
  (color, style), makeup (be specific: what look, what products/effects
  are visible), and voice: accent or nationality cues if audible, energy,
  pace.
- Exactly what they are wearing — every garment with its color,
  material, pattern, trim, and fit ("red beaded dress" is not enough —
  what pattern do the beads make, what's the neckline, what's the
  outline). Every piece of jewelry: rings, bracelets, earrings, watches —
  and the brand if identifiable.
- Style label — name the aesthetic with a real reference point
  ("Dolce & Gabbana-style baroque," "athleisure") rather than a vague
  mood word. If it matches a known style, say which.
- The setting, as it actually is — what kind of space this really
  appears to be (judge from lighting, furniture age and color, finishes —
  don't upgrade an ordinary room into an "upscale" one). Then inventory
  the room: what is on the shelves, cabinets, walls — not just the focal
  props. The whole setting is the set.
- Props — every object that appears, with color/material detail.

Then give me one table with exactly these six columns, in this order:

`Timestamp | Roll | Visuals | On-Screen Text | Transcript | Sound`

- Timestamp — start and end, e.g. `0:00 - 0:11`
- Roll — what the PICTURE is doing against the words in this row. One
  label, from this list only — it is the first thing production and the
  edit read, because it decides what has to be made and what gets laid over
  what:
  - `A` — A-roll. The person whose words we hear is on screen saying
    them. Lips visible, moving with the words.
  - `B-over` — B-roll over a speaker. The words carry straight on from
    an `A` row, in the same voice, but the picture has left the speaker to
    show something — the problem, the product, the hands, the proof. When the
    picture goes back to the speaker it is another `A` row.
  - `B-vo` — B-roll under a voice we never see. A narrator or a
    voiceover; nobody in the video is ever shown saying these words.
  - `B-silent` — B-roll with no words at all. Picture, with only music,
    sound effects or room sound under it.
  - `C` — a card. Not footage: a packshot on a plain ground, a title
    card, a full-screen graphic, a screenshot, a text-only frame.
  If the speaker is in frame but the words belong to someone else, or the
  lips are hidden the whole row, it is `B-vo`, not `A` — say who is in frame
  in Visuals. Never leave this column blank and never invent a label.
  For every `B-over`, `B-vo` and `B-silent` row, the Visuals column must
  open with what the picture is there to show in three or four plain
  words before the description — `[shows: the problem, close]`,
  `[shows: product in hand]`, `[shows: before and after]` — so the row can
  be re-made as its own shot.
- Visuals — only what is in frame, at the same level of detail as the
  profile above: who is there, what they are doing, their facial
  expression and reaction, camera work for this row — shot type, camera
  angle, front-facing or rear camera, phone-grade or high-res — setting,
  props, and effects. Every effect or transition gets its shape, size,
  color, and a canonical name you reuse every time it recurs
  (`Transition-1: pink glowing heart, ~1/3 screen height`). No on-screen
  text in this column.
- On-Screen Text — every overlay and caption, word for word, in
  order, separated by `|`. Nothing else in this column. Write "none" if
  there is none. If a text element looks like an app or editor watermark
  rather than intentional content, mark it `[ARTIFACT: …]` — we must not
  copy junk into new videos.
- Transcript — exactly what is SPOKEN, word for word. The transcript
  is never the on-screen text — if they differ, the spoken words go here
  and the overlay stays in its own column. Label every line with its
  speaker (`Creator:`, `Doctor:`, name them if named) and, where it
  carries meaning, the tone (`[skeptical]`, `[excited]`). If there is no
  speech, describe the music or sound design in brackets — and if you can
  identify the actual track, name it. If speech is inaudible or garbled,
  mark `[UNCLEAR]` — never fill the gap with the overlay text or a guess.
- Sound — everything in this row that is NOT speech, in three parts
  separated by `|`, each written out or `none`:
  `music: … | effects: … | room: …`
  - *music* — is a track playing under this row? Name it if you can;
    otherwise its feel, its tempo in a word, and whether it is rising,
    holding, dropping out or hitting a beat on a cut.
  - *effects* — every added sound, in order, tied to what it lands on:
    `whoosh on the cut in`, `pop on the caption`, `ding as the product
    appears`, `record scratch on her line`. An added sound is one that was
    put there in the edit, not one the room made.
  - *room* — the real sound of the place: tap running, product squeezed,
    a lid clicked, street noise, silence.
  The Transcript column still carries the words and, when nobody speaks,
  the one-line description it always has; this column is where the music
  and the effects are itemised so they can be re-made.

One row per frame — a change of speaker, camera setup, on-screen text, or
Roll (the picture leaving the speaker, or coming back) starts a NEW frame; never merge adjacent moments into one frame, and
never merge these columns. A speaker's single sentence that runs across
an `A` row, a `B-over` row and back to `A` is three rows, with the words
split where the picture actually changes. Frames group into scenes at the phase level
in THE SCENE MECHANICS below; this table does not change because of that
grouping.

2. THE SCENE MECHANICS

Break the video into its functional phases — the blocks of work, not the
individual rows. Name each phase for what it does to the viewer, not for
what is on screen (e.g. "Visual Destruction & Negative Bait Hook",
"Agitation / Problem Definition", "The Magic Mechanism", "Benefit Stacking
& Objection Handling", "Incentive Escalation", "Scarcity CTA").

Use ONE canonical name per technique: if the detailed analysis calls it
"the vulnerability hook," the phase-level view calls it the vulnerability
hook too — keep the high-level and detailed layers, but never two names
for one thing. Where a technique is an established term from psychology or
direct-response marketing, use the established term.

Every scene carries a section label — which part of the argument it
is doing, from the fixed list supplied at the top of this prompt. Use the
section ids exactly as that list spells them. Pick the one it actually
is; do not force a fit. If none of them describes this scene, write your
own plain description of what it is doing and mark it
`(candidate for the bank)` — a finding for a human, never a decision to add
to the list yourself.

The labels are a vocabulary, not a sequence. The asset carries whichever
sections it carries, in whatever order, with repeats — read that off the
video, never impose an order on it.

For every phase, give me all four of these as separate labeled lines:

- Phase name and timestamp range
- Mechanical purpose — one line: what this phase is engineered to do to
  the viewer.
- Why it works — the detail. Name the specific thing doing the work:
  the exact words, the visual choice, the pacing, the order. Name the
  technique it belongs to. Say what the viewer feels or concludes. Then
  end the line with the labeled clause `Breaks if cut:` followed by the
  specific thing that fails without this phase — every phase, no
  exceptions.
- What it sets up — the next phase this one makes possible.
- Section: the label from the list above (or the plain description
  plus `(candidate for the bank)`).
- The scene contract, as observed — eight labeled lines, exactly as
  the video plays it, never as it should be: `Duration:` (start–end, from
  this phase's own timestamps) · `Event:` (what physically happens, in
  order) · `Emotion:` (the one feeling the viewer carries through it) ·
  `Outcome:` (where the viewer lands by its end) · `Camera:` (operator
  behavior over the scene — height, distance, movement) · `Staging:`
  (who is where, who moves, who does not) · `Delta:` (what visibly
  changes first frame to last) · `Boundary:` (what this scene does NOT
  do, as observed — no text yet, no product yet, no resolution yet).
- Elements present — characters (by the names already fixed in the
  Character & Setting profile) · props and products · words said ·
  on-screen text · sounds · the pain or the proof it shows, if either is
  in it. An element with nothing to report says "none" rather than being
  left out.

Then two more sub-sections:

- Script beats. Every line that does real work gets its own entry —
  not a selected few, and a phase with three working lines gets three
  entries. Each entry is four labeled lines, in this order and no other
  shape:

  ```
  Line: "<the words, quoted exactly>"
  Device: <the named technique this line is performing>
  Why it lands: <why it works on the specific audience this video is
  aimed at — name who that is and what they already believe>
  Trigger words: <the individual words carrying the charge, listed>
  ```

  A one-line summary in place of the four is a failed entry. `Device` and
  `Trigger words` are never the same content: the device is what the line
  does, the trigger words are which words do it.
- Pacing and rhythm. How often it cuts, how long each phase runs, where
  the tempo shifts, and what each shift is buying.

3. THE PSYCHOLOGY

Use the same subsection structure every time, in this order — formats may
add extras at the end, but these six always appear with these names:

- The thesis — one line naming the whole psychological play, in quotes
  (e.g. "The Maverick Insider vs. The Stagnant Institution").
- Character archetype — who the presenter is being, using established
  archetype language (e.g. "the aspirational self," "the sage") rather
  than invented labels, and the specific signals that build it: wardrobe,
  styling, cadence, eye contact, props. Cite only details already in the
  objective record.
- Aesthetic archetype — what the look itself signals, and what it
  borrows credibility from.
- The setting — what the location does for believability.
- The psychological plays — numbered. Name each one — established
  terms referenced where they exist — then say how it is executed in this
  specific video. End every play with the labeled line `Belief shift:`
  naming what the viewer now believes (or no longer believes) because of
  this play — every play, no exceptions.
- The viewer's end state — what the viewer believes, feels, or fears at
  the end that they did not at 0:00.

4. THE VOICE FINGERPRINT

The record above says how the speaker *looks and sounds*. This section says
how they build a sentence — because a downstream stage writing copy in
their name has nothing else to go on, and copy that gets the argument right
and the voice wrong reads as a brand wearing a person's face.

Every line here quotes the source. No inference, no adjectives about their
"tone" — the mechanics, evidenced:

- Sentence shape. Their typical length in words, and whether they speak
  in full sentences or fragments. Quote the shortest complete thought they
  say and the longest.
- How they open a thought. Do they front the noun ("Dark, gritty, heavy
  in the hand") or narrate their way in ("The first thing I noticed
  was…")? Quote two openings.
- Their own nouns. What do they call the problem, the product, the body
  part — in their words, not the category's. If they name the problem with
  a phrase harsher than a brand would use, quote it exactly; that phrase is
  the most valuable line in the record.
- Energy and punctuation. Where do they land emphasis — exclamations,
  repetition, a flat full stop? If they repeat a structure ("my hands, my
  arms, my chest"), quote it; that rhythm is theirs.
- How they hedge, qualify or withhold. The exact words they use when
  they are NOT sure, or when they refuse to claim something. Quote it.
- Their tics. Connectives, filler, asides, the way they start a
  correction. Two or three examples, verbatim.
- How they close. Their actual sign-off, quoted — verdict, thanks,
  question to the audience, or nothing.

Then one line, labeled `Fingerprint:` — the voice in one sentence a
writer could hold in their head while writing. Not a compliment about them;
a set of constraints. *"Fragments, noun-first, refuses to claim what they
have not proven, signs off with a verdict."*

5. THE INDEX

Close with three short registries:

- Definitions index — every named technique and play used above, one
  line each: the canonical name, a one-line definition, and whether it is
  an established term (say from where) or our working label.
- Element registry — every recurring visual element (transitions,
  effects, overlays) with its canonical name, shape, size, and color, and
  the timestamps where it appears.
- Roll sheet — first, one line of counts: how many rows and how many
  seconds sit under each Roll label (`A` · `B-over` · `B-vo` · `B-silent` ·
  `C`). Then the make-list: every row that is NOT `A`, in order, one line
  each — its timestamp, its label, its `[shows: …]` tag, the first and last
  spoken words it covers (or `no words`), and its length. This is the list
  of shots someone has to go and make besides the speaker; if a row is
  missing from it, that shot never gets made.

## THE DOCTRINE READ OF THE SOURCE

The video has been torn down. This pass reads the doctrine off the source
as it was swiped and banks it, so the unique framework behind an asset
worth swiping survives the run instead of dying inside one brief.

You are reading THEIR asset, not ours. Nothing here is a decision about
the rebuild — not who it speaks to, not what it should lead with, not what
we would do differently. A later pass answers for our reader. This one
answers one question: *what is the source actually doing, in the doctrine's
own vocabulary?*

Four rules govern everything below.

Read, never improve. You are not allowed an opinion on whether the asset
is good, whether the move lands, or what it should have done instead. A
weak asset gets an accurate read, not a better one. The moment the read
describes a stronger version of the asset it has described something that
does not exist, and the bank fills with assets nobody ever made.

Cite or leave blank. Every slot points at a timestamp span or a quoted
line from the teardown record. Nothing is inferred from category habit,
from what assets of this kind usually do, or from the frameworks above. If
the record does not settle a slot, the answer is `not shown in the record`
and that is a complete answer. An uncited read is worse than a missing one,
because it is banked and reused as if it were observed.

The sections are a vocabulary, not a sequence. The asset carries
whichever sections it carries, in whatever order, with repeats. Read that
off the record; never impose an order on it and never fill a gap with the
section that "should" come next.

Never coin a name. No invented framework name, no clever label, no
"the X play". A framework is either a row of the crosswalk in the section
list, spelled as that list spells it, or a plain description of the shape
in ordinary words marked `(no crosswalk row)`. A coined name is a thing
nobody can look up, and the bank is only useful if two people reading it a
month apart mean the same thing.

## THE SLOTS

Print these in this order, one label per line, spelled exactly as written
here. Later stages and the bank both key off these labels — a label
reworded, renumbered or wrapped in a sentence is a label nothing can find.

FRAMEWORK: the shape the whole asset follows. A crosswalk row from the
section list, or a plain description of the shape marked
`(no crosswalk row)`. One line, then one line of why, citing the beats that
make it that shape rather than a neighbouring one.

CROSSWALK ROW: the exact row name from the crosswalk if the asset
follows one, or `none` if it does not. This is the machine-readable half of
the line above; the two never disagree.

SECTIONS CARRIED: the section ids in the order the asset plays them,
repeats included, each with its timestamp span — `hook 0:00–0:04` — and
nothing else on the line. Use the ids exactly as the section list spells
them. A stretch of the asset that no section describes is written as your
own plain description of what it is doing, marked
`(candidate for the bank)`, and keeps its span like every other entry.

AWARENESS ENTRY: which of the five levels the source's own viewer is
entered at, spelled as the awareness slice spells it, plus the beat that
proves it — what the asset explains or defines is what its viewer is not
assumed to know, and what it mentions with no setup is what it assumes.

AWARENESS EXIT: the level the viewer is standing on when the asset
ends, spelled the same way, plus the beat that proves it. Entry is not
destination: an asset that opens on the problem and closes on the offer has
walked the viewer up the ladder, and both ends are facts about the asset.
Where the asset never moves anyone, entry and exit are the same level and
you say so plainly.

SOPHISTICATION SIGNATURE: which stage-move the asset runs — the plain
claim, the claim enlarged, a mechanism, the mechanism enlarged, or
identification only. One of those five, cited to the asset's first working
beat. This is what the asset leads with and where the weight of its
argument sits, not everything it eventually mentions: an asset that states
a benefit and explains the how in passing later is not running a mechanism.

MASS DESIRE: the one desire the asset rides, in the source's own words
— quote them — and the beat they come from. One desire, never a list; an
asset that seems to ride several is riding one and decorating with the
rest, and the lead is the one the opening beat picks.

DESIRE URGENCY: how the asset shows the want demanding satisfaction
now rather than eventually, cited — or `not shown in the record`.

DESIRE STAYING POWER: how the asset shows the want returning after it
has been satisfied once, cited — or `not shown in the record`.

DESIRE SCOPE: how the asset shows how much of its market carries the
want at all, cited — or `not shown in the record`.

TECHNIQUES OBSERVED: per section carried, which of the seven the asset
visibly uses and which sub-method, with the span. One line per
observation:

```
<section id> · <technique> · <sub-method> · <span> · <the beat that shows it>
```

Name the technique and the sub-method exactly as the technique slice spells
them. `none observed` is a valid and common answer for a section — say it
rather than reaching for the technique the section list associates with
that section, which is what the section is *built from* in general, never
evidence about this asset.

MOOD: the register the asset runs on — the rhythm and word choice, in
the mood slice's own terms — and every point where it shifts, with the
span. Rhythm is read off the transcript and the pacing rows in the record:
short stacked lines and long plain ones do different work.

THE DELIVERY READ: how the source is PERFORMED, one labelled line each,
spelled with the values the delivery dials above carry and nothing else.
Read it off the record — the transcript, the pacing rows, what the record
says is on camera — and cite the timestamp span that shows it. A dial the
record does not settle is `not shown in the record`, which is a complete
answer here exactly as it is everywhere else in this pass.

```
HUMOR: <one value from the humor dial> · <span> · <the beat that shows it>
DELIVERY STYLE: <one value from the delivery-style dial> · <span> · <the beat>
REGISTER: <one value from the register dial> · <span> · <the beat>
PACING: <one value from the pacing dial> · <the shape across the whole asset>
REFERENCE WORLD: <what the asset VISIBLY leans on — a creator's format, a
show's cadence, a meme shape, a wardrobe or a sound world — one per line,
each with its span. Only what the record shows. Never a guess at what the
maker watched.>
AVOID: <what the asset plainly does NOT do — the register it stays out of,
the ad-voice tells it strips — each cited to the absence you can point at,
or `not shown in the record`.>
```

Four things this slot may not do. It may not name a real person as a
likeness or a voice — a reference is a direction for timing, cadence and
world, and the reading says what the asset LEANS ON, never who it sounds
like. It may not reach for a dial value because the format usually runs on
it; the record is the only evidence. It may not read taste off our own
avatar — this is the source's delivery, not a plan for ours; the creative
pass decides later what to keep and what to swap. And it may not invent a
value: every answer is a value the dials above carry, spelled the way they
spell it, or `not shown in the record`.

FORMAT: which ONE row of the video-format list above the source is — the
row's `id`, spelled exactly as the list spells it — then ` · ` and the span
or beat that shows it. A format is what KIND of video this is, read off what
the record says is on screen and how it is staged. If no row describes the
source, write your own plain description of what kind of video it is, marked
`(no format row)` — never the nearest row, and never a coined name.

STRUCTURE: which ONE row of the video-structure list above the source
follows — the row's `id`, spelled exactly as the list spells it — then ` · `
and the spans that make it that structure rather than a neighbouring one. A
structure is the ORDER OF MOVES the video runs, independent of its subject.
If no row describes it, write the order of moves in plain words, marked
`(no structure row)`. A source that fits no row is a finding, and the bank

## WHAT IS NEVER SUBSTITUTED

Substitution replaces the thing being sold. It does not replace the thing
being worn, revealed, or laughed at.

### The failure this exists to stop

The source: a 70-year-old talks for 35 seconds about a red string swimsuit a
relative left behind, then walks through a doorway wearing it, holds both
arms out, pulls a horrified face, and retreats. The swimsuit is the setup, the
wardrobe, and the punchline.

The injection swapped the swimsuit for a tube of the brand's own product — the
object held up, the thing that "didn't work for me" — and then wrote:

> *"The speaker walks through the doorway in a sleeveless top or short sleeves,
> arms bare, having previously been covered. Structurally identical to the
> source's swimsuit walk-through."*

It is not structurally identical. The laugh is a person of that age walking out
in a string swimsuit. Changing into a short-sleeved top is getting dressed. The
video kept its shape and lost the only reason anyone watched to the end, and
every pass downstream inherited a version with no climax and reported fine.

### The rule

Before substituting any object, ask: is this object worn, revealed, or
reacted to on camera?

- If it is only referred to or held up — a bottle, a jar, a gadget on
  a counter — substitute freely. That is the job.
- If it is worn, or the camera's reaction to it is the payoff — it stays.
  Every time. The swimsuit stays a swimsuit. The dress stays a dress. The
  wardrobe change stays a wardrobe change.

Some products cannot be worn at all. When the source's object cannot
physically be our product, that is not a licence to change the object — it is
the signal that the object was never the swap.

### Where the product goes instead

It enters after the beat, as what the speaker does about what the reveal
exposed. They still walk out in the swimsuit. The camera still finds what the
reveal exposed on their arms and legs. That is our problem statement, delivered
by the source's own punchline — stronger than anything we would write, because
it is already funny and the speaker is already in on it.

Follow the source until the product has a reason to exist. On organic sources
that reason almost always arrives *after* the payoff, never in place of it.

### The check before this pass ends

Find the beat with the biggest visual reaction in it. Read our version of that
beat. If a stranger would not laugh, wince or lean in at the same moment the
source made them, the substitution has eaten the asset. Put the object back.

## THE CONNECTIVE TISSUE HAS TO SOUND LIKE A PERSON

Added at v5. On an organic source there is no commercial spine to inherit, so
this pass writes the lines that carry a viewer from their world into the product.
Those written-from-nothing lines are the ones that come back sounding like an
advert read aloud, and one flat line is enough to make the whole take feel
staged.

The test: read the line out loud in the speaker's register. If you would not say it to
someone standing in your kitchen, it does not go in.

What that rules out, specifically:

- Marketing connectives. "That's when I discovered", "here's the thing",
  "little did I know", "and that's why", "the secret is", "game changer".
  Nobody says these. They are the seam showing.
- The pivot sentence. A line whose only job is to turn the video toward the
  product — "so when I found something that actually worked…". If the turn
  needs a sentence to explain itself, the turn is in the wrong place. Move it.
- Explaining the joke. On a comedy source, the funny beat lands and then a
  line arrives telling the viewer what it meant. Cut that line every time.
- Stacked adjectives. "Rich, creamy, luxurious." Real speech uses one, and
  usually a plain one.
- Symmetry. "Not just X, but Y." "It's not about A. It's about B." Written
  rhythm, not spoken rhythm.
- Reading the product's own words back. A benefit lifted off the product
  file, spoken as though the speaker thought of it.

What it sounds like instead:

- Half-sentences and restarts. People stop and change direction mid-thought.
  A line that reads slightly broken usually plays as real.
- The specific over the general. Not "my skin felt rough" — "the backs of
  my arms felt like a cat's tongue."
- Understatement at the moment of the claim. The strongest line in a real
  video is usually the flattest one.
- The avatar's own words first. The language bank is verbatim customer speech. Use a
  phrase from it rather than writing a new one that means the same thing.

One check before this pass ends: find the line where the video turns toward
the product. Read it aloud. If it sounds like an advert, rewrite it as the
thing the speaker would actually say, or cut it and let the turn happen visually.

What a SPOKEN paragraph obeys — every line of it, no exceptions:

0. THE UNIT IS THE THOUGHT, NOT THE SENTENCE (Damon's ruling, 2026-09-19,
   after hearing a paragraph read as three clipped statements: "it doesn't
   feel like one complete sentence like it actually should"). A person says
   one thought in one breath, and the punctuation marks where they breathe,
   not where a grammar book would cut. Never chop one thought into a row of
   short statements — that reads as a list, and a list is not a person.
   Keep the thought whole and mark the breathing: an ellipsis (`...`) where
   the speaker pauses mid-thought before finishing it; a full stop where the
   thought ends; an exclamation or a question where the voice lifts because
   the point lands; a comma for a breath that does not stop. The reference,
   Damon's own rewrite of the same line — prose: *This — the old surface
   still sitting on your hand — is why the sunscreen hasn't worked.* Chopped,
   wrong: *This is the old surface. It's still sitting on your hand. That's
   why the sunscreen hasn't worked.* Spoken, right: This is the old surface
   still sitting on your hand... That's why the sunscreen hasn't worked!
   The test: read the SPOKEN paragraph aloud once. A full stop inside a
   thought a person would finish in one breath is a chop — join it back with
   a comma or an ellipsis. A thought that ends flat where the person would
   lean on it is missing its lift.
1. No em-dash, no en-dash, no semicolon, no parenthesis, no bracket. The
   ellipsis is KEPT — it is the mid-thought breath. Where the prose used a
   dash to hang a thought, the thought stays in the same breath: a comma
   carries it or an ellipsis marks the pause. The brief's gate refuses the
   dash, the semicolon and the parenthesis by name, so a paragraph that still
   carries one is a paragraph that will not ship.
2. One thought per breath. A breath that carries two thoughts is two
   breaths. Fragments are allowed where a person would use one, as part of
   a thought, never as a way of chopping one up: "Not the sunscreen. The
   surface under it."
3. Contractions on, at every level above `0 plain`: it's, that's,
   doesn't, isn't, you're, I've. At `2 casual` and above the reduced forms
   the register recipe allows (gonna, wanna, kinda) are allowed where the
   rows show the room uses them.
4. The room's own fillers and discourse markers — so, look, honestly, I
   mean, right, okay, anyway — only from the filler set the register recipe
   names for this delivery style, narrowed to the markers the voiceprints
   measured for this avatar's creators where those exist (a marker the
   creators never say is not this room's, whatever the recipe allows), and
   only from the words the `in-word` and `community-voice` rows carry at
   level 3 and above. Never more than the recipe's density. A filler
   stacked on a filler is a tell.
5. Direct address. The viewer is "you". A generic third person in the
   prose ("the one who checks") becomes "you" where the sense survives it.
6. Questions to the viewer are allowed where the recipe allows them, and
   a question is answered in the next sentence, never left hanging past the
   scene.
7. One restart per paragraph at most ("It's not, okay, it's not the
   sunscreen."), and only where the recipe's restart line permits it.
8. The meaning is byte-identical in sense. Every claim, number, product
   name, mechanism step and offer term survives. A claim that got shorter
   still says the same thing; a claim that got vaguer is a defect. Read each
   SPOKEN paragraph back against its BEFORE and confirm nothing was added
   and nothing was lost.
9. Printed as the model reads it. The punctuation map above is the only

   the breath, a question mark lifts, and nothing else is written in. No
   stage directions inside the paragraph, no speaker label, no tag the model
   we use does not honour.

    and no row behind it is the writer's taste, and it is reverted.

## The opening section — the part that gets mangled

- `Say:` the spoken line, word for word. If the format has no speech,

The scroll stoppers are already written. You are copying them, not writing
them. The hook set hands you each one as a described frame. Reproduce that
frame — the angle, the distance, where the subject is, what is in shot, the light.

This is the single thing that goes wrong most. On one run the source was a
woman lying on a towel on a beach in midday sun, the hook set said exactly
that, and the brief opened on a bathroom counter in the morning instead. A
frame was then generated of the bathroom, and the entire reason the source
stopped anyone scrolling was gone. The scroll stopper is the swipe. It is
the most valuable thing in the teardown and the least replaceable.

- Never relocate the opening. If the source's first frame is outdoors, the
  opening is outdoors. Do not move it somewhere easier to shoot.
- Never substitute a product shot for a scroll stopper. A tube held up to
  camera is not an attention device; it is what the source spent its opening
  earning the right to show.
- Never paraphrase it into a summary. "Relaxing outside" is not the
  frame. The angle, the distance and what fills the frame are the frame.
- Frame 1 of the shot list IS the control's scroll stopper, written out
  as a frame with its wardrobe line. The two must describe the same picture.
  If Frame 1 and the control disagree, Frame 1 is wrong.

shot by shot, and the opening frame is in its first row. All hooks get filmed;

## THE UNIT IS THE FRAME — AND THE SCRIPT DECIDES HOW MANY

Every frame exists because a line or a beat in the script calls for it.
One shot, one frame. No frame without something to carry.

Two different sources come through here and they are not the same job.

A source that is already a finished ad is a format to replicate. Its
shots are the plan. Transcribe them: the teardown's Visuals column, split
where a row holds several shots — "Creator in kitchen. Cut to daughter
on sofa. Close-up of the button" is three shots — and that count is your
count. Writing more means inventing footage nobody filmed.

A source that is an organic post is a hook, not an ad. It is a few
seconds long and the ad you are briefing is not. The close pass hands you
the full script; your shot list covers all of it, to the last line. The
source's shots cover the opening and then run out, and where they run out
you keep going — the script is still talking, so there are still frames.

### Count against the script, not the clock

Before writing, read the script the close pass produced and count what it
needs: every spoken line, every card, every held beat. That is your frame
count. Then check the teardown: wherever the source has a shot for that
moment, the frame is the source's shot. Wherever it does not, the frame
comes from what the line needs — plainly, one shot, nothing invented around
it.

Finishing at the source's runtime when the script runs longer is the failure
this rule exists to stop. A run on 2026-08-26 briefed a nine-second post as
a nine-second ad: seven frames, no body, nothing after the hook. The script
was there and the shot list stopped short of it.

### Why the opposite rule exists too

A run on 2026-08-25 turned a source describing eighteen shots into a brief
of forty-one frames, on a source that was already a finished ad. The extra
frames had nothing to describe, so each was filled with plausible invention:
a wardrobe change nobody called for, a mortar and pestle nobody filmed.
Every fault in that brief lived in a frame that should not have existed.

Both failures are the same mistake — the frame count coming from somewhere
other than the script. Invented frames are not extra value; a shot list that
stops before the script does is not restraint.

### What "every shot" includes

If the source cuts to a shop, you have a shop. If it cuts to B-roll of
somewhere abroad, you have that B-roll. If it goes back to the keyed talking
head for one line, that is a frame too. A shot you find awkward to shoot
is still a shot — describe it and let the maker or the owner decide. Never
silently drop one because it is inconvenient; a dropped shot is as wrong as
an invented one, and it is the reason a swiped format stops working.

### Do not write a transcript at the end

The finished brief ends with every spoken line laid out in order, so a
creator can read the script without hunting through a shot list for it.
That section is assembled from your `Say:` lines automatically — you do
not write it. Writing your own would produce a second version of the
script that drifts from the frames. Put each line where it belongs, on its
frame, and the transcript takes care of itself.

## HOW THE FRAME IS PUT TOGETHER

A frame is not always one plain camera shot, and when it is not, say so
first — this is the part that makes a format reproducible. Read the
teardown record's own description and carry the construction across:

- A keyed or composited frame — she is filmed against a background that
  is not the room she is in, cut out and laid over other footage. Say what
  she occupies, and what is playing behind her.
- Picture-in-picture — her talking head in a corner over a full-frame
  plate. Say which corner and roughly how much of the frame she takes.
- Split screen — say how it divides and what is on each side.
- A screen recording — a phone or a browser being captured. Say what is
  on the screen.
- A plain shot — say nothing about construction; just describe it.

Formats live or die on this. Reproducing the words of a keyed talking-head
opener as a plain selfie throws away the thing that made it work.

## Every frame

Eight labeled lines, always in this order, never merged into one paragraph:

1. `Frame N · start–end` — bold, the word Frame, number, middle dot, span.
2. `Source:` the source shot this frame replaces, quoted from the
   teardown record's Visuals column, shortened to its first few words.
   Frame N replaces source shot N — the order never changes.

   This line is what makes the brief checkable. Anyone can read down the
   two columns and see instantly whether the shot list still tracks the
   video it came from. Without it, one dropped shot silently shifts
   everything after it by one and the second half of the brief quietly
   stops being the format at all — which is exactly what happened on
   2026-08-25, where frame 16 was a pair of before-and-after photographs
   standing where the source had a woman walking into a restaurant.

   If you find yourself unable to write this line for a frame, that frame
   has no source shot and should not exist.
3. `Film:` how the frame is built (above) and then what the maker does —
   where they are, the light, what is in their hands, what they do with it.
   This is direction for the camera. It never contains a line she says out
   loud — a spoken line always goes under `Say:`, never folded into the
   filming direction.
4. `Hear:` what the moment sounds like — the room tone, the thing that
   makes a noise, the silence when it drops out. One short line.

   Only what the maker can actually capture in the room she is in. No music
   direction, no sound design, no effect she would have to go and source. If
   the honest answer is that the moment is nearly silent, write that —
   "nothing but the room" is a real instruction, and often the strongest one
   on the page.

   Take it from what is physically happening in the frame, never from
   atmosphere you imagine. A box being opened makes a noise; a tap running
   makes a noise; a room full of people makes a noise. The teardown record
   only describes sound where nobody is speaking, so for every other scene
   you are reasoning from the objects and actions already in the shot — not
   inventing a mood. If nothing in the frame would make a sound worth
   naming, say so and stop.
5. `Wearing:` on every single frame, spelled out in full — garment,
   colour, sleeves, hair, jewellery. Never "as before", never "same as frame 2",
   never left out. A frame with no wardrobe line is a broken frame: the picture
   for it gets generated from nothing and comes back as a stranger in clothes
   the subject does not own.

   The outfits are the source's, and there are only as many as it has.
   The teardown record lists the wardrobe — usually one main outfit and
   occasionally a second for a specific scene. Use those and no others.
   Write the same outfit, word for word, on every consecutive frame until
   the source itself changes clothes.

   A change of clothes is a change of time or place, and it must be
   visible in the story. She is in her main outfit narrating; she is in
   the second outfit at the event. If you cannot point at the moment in the
   source where she changed, she did not change — repeat the previous
   wardrobe line exactly. Outfits drifting frame to frame for no reason is
   the single clearest sign a brief was invented rather than observed, and
   it reads as slop to anyone who watches it back.
6. `On screen:` the words burned into the picture at this moment, copied
   verbatim from the teardown record's own On-Screen Text column, in
   quotes. This is the caption the viewer reads, and it is very often not
   the same as what she is saying — it lags, it shortens, it stops
   mid-sentence. Copy what the source actually put on screen; never tidy it
   into a full sentence and never re-derive it from the spoken line.

   If the record shows nothing on screen for this frame, write
   `On screen: nothing`. That is a real instruction — it tells the editor to
   leave the picture clean.

   A frame can have both a caption and speech at the same time, and most
   of these formats do. An earlier version of this prompt said a scene had
   one or the other; that was wrong and it lost every caption in the brief.
7. On its own line, as a markdown quote (`> `): `> Say:` followed
   by the exact words she speaks on camera, in quotes — the voiceover,
   nothing else. If nobody speaks in this frame, write
   `> Say: nothing — picture only`. Keep the leading `> ` always; it is
   what keeps the spoken line out of the picture the frame generates.
8. `![Frame N](—)` on its own line.

## EVERY FRAME STANDS ALONE — IT CANNOT SEE THE ONE BEFORE IT

Each frame is read on its own, by someone who has not read any other
frame. So every Film line names the place, and what the body is doing, in
full — every time, even when nothing has changed since the last frame.

Continuation shorthand is the failure. "You stay exactly where you are",
"same as before", "back out to the seated wide", "same frame" — these are
references, and there is nothing there to refer to. A frame reading "you
stay exactly where you are, hands behind your head" put a woman standing on
a wooden deck in the middle of a shoot that happens entirely on a beach
(2026-08-26), because the only thing the line actually said was "hands
behind your head".

Write it as if it were the first frame. "Lying on the white towel on the
sand, hands behind your head, nothing moving." Repeating the place on
every frame is not clutter — it is the only thing holding the set together.

Where the camera goes is not what the picture shows. "Phone propped on
the counter", "lens twelve inches off the ground", "tripod at chest height"
are instructions for the person filming, and they belong in the line — but
put them at the END, after the picture is described, never as the opening
words. Lead with what is in the shot.

## THE FILM LINE HAS TO BE SEEABLE

This applies to the shot list only — never to the opening. The hooks and
their scroll stoppers arrive already written and you copy them exactly, as
the rule above says. Sharpening a scroll stopper is rewriting it. If the
opening reads flat, that is a finding about the hook set, not licence to
improve it here — say so and move on.

For the body frames, which you do write: a frame description is not an
inventory. "Bathroom vanity, cluttered" tells the maker nothing she can
point a camera at. Write the frame so someone who has never seen the source
can see it. You have the teardown record — the source shot by shot — so the
detail is there to be used, not invented.

For every `Film:` line, name at least one of:

- the specific physical detail that makes it real — the cake going soft at
  the edges, the sponge coming away brown, the cuff pulled down over the
  knuckles
- what the light is doing — hard summer light, flat white light, one window
  on her left
- the movement that carries the beat — the tug, the pinch, the hand stopping

Keep it to what a phone can capture in a room she already has.

This is description, not invention. You are describing the frame the
source already earned, in sharper focus. You are not adding a prop, a
location, a person, or an action that was not there. If you find yourself
writing a shot the source did not have, stop — that is a different job and
it breaks the swipe.

## THE CONVERGENCE CHECK

Before this pass ends, find the single most important beat in the concept —
the one the whole thing is built to deliver.

Read its three lines together: `Film:`, `Hear:`, `Say:`.

They must point at the same thing at the same moment. Picture, sound and
words converging is what carries a beat into the part of a viewer's
attention where it means something. The same three pulling in different
directions is where briefs go slack.

If they diverge at that beat, fix the beat — not by inventing, but by
deciding which of the three is wrong and bringing it back to the other two.

## WHAT SHE MAY NOT HAVE

An opening or a frame can ask for something that is not hers to supply: a
prop she may not own, a second person, a photograph someone sent her, a
place, a fact about her life. The shot stays — dropping it breaks the
swipe — but it never goes out as a demand.

Directly under any such opening or frame, one line, as a markdown quote,
always an out and never a question back to us:

> If you don't have this: skip this opening and shoot the other five.

> If you don't have this: use whatever you actually put on to cover
> them, and film that.

> Only if it's true for you. If it isn't, say the first half and leave
> the rest out.

The maker decides in the room; nothing waits on an answer. Fifteen briefs
went out on 2026-09-02 asking for cover-up makeup, a self-tanner, a drawer
of failed products, a phone photo from a daughter and a young woman under
studio light, with two notes like this across the whole set — and the
creators wrote back asking what they were supposed to do.

# WHAT DOES NOT GO IN

- No source link, no reference lines or timestamps into anyone else's footage.
- No "Don't say" list, no compliance language, no guarantee wording. That is
  our review before anything ships, not something the maker needs on the page.
- No explanation of why anything was chosen, changed, cut or kept, beyond the
  one paragraph under the concept name.
- No runtime totals, beat counts or budgets.
- No sign-off, no thanks, no lengthy introduction.

# THE CLOSE

Two things, in this order.

First, the AI notice. Print it exactly as written, word for word, immediately
before the questions line. It is a promise being made to the creator about her
own likeness, so it is not yours to paraphrase, soften, move or leave out —
and it belongs in the brief itself, not only in whatever document the brief is

Then one line:

Nothing else follows it.

# TONE

Short sentences. Address the maker directly. Every scene reads in three
seconds flat: what to film, what it sounds like, what to wear, what is on
screen, what to say — nothing to parse.

# WHAT AMPLIFICATION MEANS HERE

You may make the same thing land harder. You may not make a different thing.

Free — how it is delivered:

- the rhythm and syntax of a spoken line
- how vividly a frame is described
- which physical detail gets named

Locked — what is being said:

- the beats, their order, and the structure they sit in
- every claim, number, price, offer, guarantee and timeline
- which product does what, and what is in it
- who is on camera, and what they are wearing

The test: if a change alters what a viewer learns, it is out of scope.
If it only alters how hard that lands, it is the job.

## Nothing new

Every line traces to the spec, the product file, the offer file or the
creator profile. You are choosing and compressing, never writing. If it is
not in the spec, it is not in the concept: no beat, no hook, no claim, no
prop, no place, no person, no number.

## Short is the whole point

The concept block is 180 to 280 words before the script. Five beats — never
more, never fewer than four. Two hooks, three at most. Four to six shots.
Two or three product points. If the spec has thirteen beats, six openings
and forty lines of script, that is the spec's job; here it is five beats and
two hooks. Where several frames are one action, that is one beat. Where a
beat is a sentence she says, the beat says what she says in a few words, not
the line.

## Hooks are directions, not lines to read

A hook goes in as a short quoted sentence she can say her own way — the
control opening first. Pick the second for what it needs the least: a hook
that needs a prop, a second person, an old photograph or a place she may not
have is skipped in favour of one that needs only her. Never six. The page
does not explain that we test hooks; it just offers two.

## Beats, not cuts

Written to her — "you open in the car", "you show the tube" — the way the
first brief was, never "she" or "the creator". One beat per thing that happens: open, the problem or the story, the
product enters, how it is used or what it does, the close. No timestamps, no
frame numbers, no durations, no camera directions, no wardrobe, no light.
Where the spec names a second person on camera (a mother, a friend, a
child), the first beat says so plainly by role — and the Format line carries
"with ⟨role⟩" so she sees it before she reads on.

## Must-have shots are things, not instructions

Each shot is a noun phrase she can tick off: "Direct-to-camera opening in
your car", "Both products shown clearly", "Close-up of the scrub on damp
skin", "Final product shot". The spec's most important beat is always the
first one listed. Nothing about colour, garment, jewellery, window or wall.

## Product points are hers to choose

Plain sentences about what the product does and how it is used, from the
spec's spoken lines and the product file. If the guarantee appears in the
spec's lines it goes in as its exact words, once; if a price appears, it may
go in; neither is required. The heading line "Choose the points that best
reflect your real experience:" stays word for word.

## The script comes after you

No "say these", no locked lines, no filler in the concept itself. The whole
script, in order, one line per beat, is lifted from the spec by the machine
and placed under its own heading after your Product Points — she reads it
as-is or says it her way (Damon, 2026-09-18: "we still want the script").
You do not write it; your concept ends at the last product point.

## No pictures

No image line anywhere. The AI pictures leave the document: they go to her
as a separate inspiration file in the same folder, built by the machine
from the spec. A picture inside the brief is what made it read as a shot
list.

## The heading and the product

The heading is `# ⟨the concept name⟩` — a top-level heading, no number,
because this document holds one concept and the heading is its title. The
product is named by its exact name from the product file, on the Format
lines, once. Never a product the spec does not show.
