# What the spend says

The strategy lesson, read straight off nine competitors' live ad accounts.

Nine brands. **6,269 ads running right now**, spread across **600 different
pitches**. Every number below comes from that sweep. Where the numbers cannot
answer a question, it says so instead of guessing.

**How to read "a pitch".** A pitch is one distinct argument — one headline plus
the body copy under it. A brand running 800 ads on one pitch means 800 separate
ads carrying the same argument, cut differently: different footage, different
faces, different edits, same sell. Ads is the unit we can count. Money is not.

Read the table top to bottom and the whole strategy lesson is already visible:
**the brands running the most ads are running the fewest ideas.**

---

## 1. Concentration: they bet almost everything on three pitches

"Share on top 3" is the percentage of a brand's live ads sitting on its three
biggest pitches.

- The supplement brand runs **8 pitches in total** and puts **91%** of its 496
  ads on three of them.
- The Italian men's skincare brand: **83%** of 463 ads on three pitches.
- The foundation brand: **77%** of 1,830.
- The shampoo brand: **76%** of 1,901.

Four brands, four different markets, all landing between 76% and 91%. That is
not a coincidence, and it is not a house style. It is what an ad account looks
like after the losers have been switched off.

The other end tells the same story from the other side. The probiotic brand puts
only **7%** of its ads on its top three pitches — because it is running **106
pitches across just 150 ads**. It has not found the winner yet. The chocolate
brand is in the same position: **40 pitches, 44 ads, 16%**.

**The pattern: low concentration means still searching. High concentration means
found it and pouring.** The brands in between are mid-transition — the
testosterone brand at 42% across 109 pitches is clearly scaling two or three
winners while still testing widely underneath.

**What to do with it.** If you are new, you are supposed to look like the bottom
of that table — many pitches, few ads each. That is the search. The mistake is
staying there. The moment one pitch pulls away, the job changes from *inventing*
to *multiplying*, and the four brands at the top of the table are what
multiplying looks like.

**What this cannot tell you.** We count ads, not dollars. A brand could in
principle run 800 ads on a pitch and spend almost nothing on them. Nothing in
this data rules that out. What makes it unlikely is that producing and
maintaining hundreds of ads costs real money and effort, and no one keeps doing
that behind a pitch that is losing. Treat concentration as strong evidence, not
as a receipt.

## 2. How many versions they build of a winner

Take each brand's single biggest pitch and count the ads on it:

| Market | Ads on its single biggest pitch | As a share of that brand's whole account |
|---|---|---|
| Colour-adapting foundation | **924** | 51% |
| Men's hair-thinning shampoo | **835** | 44% |
| Garlic & oregano supplements | **313** | 63% |
| Italian men's face treatment | **259** | 56% |
| Men's testosterone supplement | 106 | 27% |
| Teen acne skincare | 97 | 12% |
| Nordic anti-ageing skincare | 26 | 17% |
| Women's intimate probiotic | 4 | 3% |
| No-added-sugar chocolate | 3 | 7% |

The midpoint across all nine is **106 ads on the top pitch**. Among the four
biggest advertisers it never drops below 259.

Say that plainly: **one argument, written once, built more than nine hundred
separate times.** The foundation brand's biggest pitch — a short piece about a
foundation that adjusts to your skin tone, three benefit lines, a "see how it
works" tap — is running as 924 different ads simultaneously.

The ratio in the last column of the first table says the same thing per pitch
rather than per winner. The supplement brand builds **62 ads for every pitch it
runs**. The foundation brand, 61. The shampoo brand, 49. The chocolate brand
builds **1.1** — it writes a new pitch nearly every time it makes an ad.

**What to do with it.** When a pitch works, the next move is not a new pitch. It
is the same pitch again: different face, different setting, different opening
second, different edit, same argument underneath. That is where the volume comes
from, and it is why these brands can run thousands of ads without running
thousands of ideas.

**What this cannot tell you.** We cannot see whether those 924 ads are 924
genuinely different films or a smaller number of films re-cut and re-uploaded
across placements and audiences. Both happen in real accounts. Either way the
strategic point holds — the budget is behind one argument — but do not read the
number as 924 shoots.

## 3. Video versus still image

Across all nine brands: **4,198 video ads (67%) and 2,071 still images (33%)**.
Two thirds video overall.

But the split swings enormously by brand, and the swing is informative:

- Italian men's face treatment: **96% video**
- Garlic & oregano supplements: **90% video**
- Women's intimate probiotic: **88% video**
- Colour-adapting foundation: **78% video**
- Nordic anti-ageing skincare: **35% video** (99 of its 153 ads are stills)
- Teen acne skincare: **19% video** (681 of its 837 ads are stills)

The teen acne brand is the odd one out, and worth staring at. It runs **162 pitches — more
than any other brand here — and 81% of its ads are still images.** Those two
facts go together. A still image is cheap and fast to make, so a brand doing a
lot of *searching* can afford to search in stills. The Nordic skincare brand,
second-highest on pitch count relative to its size, is the second-most
image-heavy at 65%.

Meanwhile the heavy concentrators lean on video: 96%, 90%, 78%, 66%.

So the rough rule the data supports is **stills for finding, video for
scaling** — and it holds for six of the nine brands. Two break it: the probiotic
brand is 88% video while still clearly searching, and the chocolate brand is 82%
video on a 44-ad account. Both are the smallest books here, which may mean they
are newer and simply built video-first from the start. That is a plausible
reading, not something the data confirms.

**What to do with it.** If you are testing pitches, stills let you test ten
arguments for the cost of one film, and the teen acne brand's 162 pitches are
proof that works at scale. Once a pitch wins, video is where the volume goes.

## 4. What the funnel shape suggests

The sweep does not show landing pages, so this section is inference from the
copy, and it is labelled as such throughout.

**Two clearly different sales routes are visible.**

*Route one — straight to the product page.* Some brands' biggest pitches are
product specs: a question, a list of ticked attributes, a discount. The
supplement brand is the purest case — **462 of its 496 ads (93%)** are this
shape, built as a headline, a benefit checklist, a price. The testosterone brand
puts its offer in the **headline itself** on its second, eighth and tenth
biggest pitches ("50% off plus free gifts" and similar). Copy written this way
has nowhere to go except a page where you can buy. Nothing here is a story; it
is a shelf label.

*Route two — through an article first.* Other brands' biggest pitches open like
posts, not ads: a personal account of what somebody tried and what failed, a
study with a named researcher, a forum-style confession. The intimate-health
brand runs almost nothing else — its entire visible book is first-person
accounts and "here is what I found out" pieces, with no product description in
the ad at all. The Nordic skincare brand's second-biggest pitch is a study
write-up. The acne brand's fifth is a long piece about a prescription drug.

Copy like that has to land somewhere it can finish; a product page would break
the spell two lines in. **The strong inference is an advertorial or a long read
before the product page.** Strong, but an inference — we cannot see the pages.

**A second tell: how hard they discount.** Across these accounts, discounts of
50%, 60%, 66% and 67% appear as headline offers, and one supplement sits at
$29.99 against a $59.99 list. You cannot lose half your price on a first order
unless something recovers it later — a subscription, a bundle, or a high enough
basket. That points at repeat-purchase economics rather than one-off sales. It
does not tell you which of those three it is.

**A third tell: guarantee length.** The guarantees offered are **90 days**
(testosterone supplement, hair shampoo), **60 days** (teen acne, Italian
skincare) and **30 days** (garlic supplement). Those are results-curve lengths.
A brand offers ninety days when the customer will not be able to tell whether it
worked for about ninety days — hair and hormones — and thirty when the promise
lands faster. The guarantee is doing the work of "this takes a while, stay with
it" without having to say it.

**What the data cannot tell you at all.** Not spend. Not cost per sale, return
on ad spend, or any performance figure. Not how long any ad has been running, so
a big pitch could in principle be a big new bet rather than a proven winner. Not
which page any ad leads to. Not whether the same creative is counted more than
once across placements. Every conclusion above is drawn from the *shape and
volume* of what is live — which is a genuinely good signal, and is not the same
thing as their results.

---

## The whole lesson, in five lines

1. Winning ad accounts are **narrow**: three pitches carry 76–91% of the ads.
2. Weak or early accounts are **wide**: 106 pitches across 150 ads means still
   looking.
3. When a pitch wins, they **build it 100 to 900 more times** rather than
   writing a new one.
4. **Stills to find it, video to scale it** — six of nine brands follow this.
5. **The offer lives in the ad when the ad sells directly**, and disappears from
   the ad when a page is doing the selling.

## Two smaller shapes, noted but not templated

These recur across brands but sit on too few ads to earn their own file. Worth
knowing:

**Buying for someone else** — the ad speaks to a parent, partner or adult child
rather than the person with the problem. The teen acne brand runs it at scale
(a pitch to mothers on 57 ads, one to parents on 19); the shampoo brand runs a
son-buys-for-his-father story on 24; the chocolate brand runs a mother packing a
lunchbox. **100 ads across 3 brands.** It works because the buyer's worry is
different from the sufferer's — safety and "will this hurt them" rather than
speed.

**The trend hijack** — attaching the product to a wave that is already moving.
All three appearances here attach to GLP-1 weight-loss drugs: the testosterone
brand on what rapid weight loss does to hormones (18 ads), the foundation brand
on what it does to your face (10), the shampoo brand on hair in the drain (20).
**48 ads across 3 brands, none of which sell a weight-loss product.** It works
because it reaches people who are already alarmed and already searching, and it
costs nothing to attach to. It also expires when the wave does.
