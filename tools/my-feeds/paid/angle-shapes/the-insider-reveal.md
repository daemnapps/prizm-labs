# The insider reveal

**155 ads across 5 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Teen acne skincare | 58 |
| Colour-adapting foundation, women 45+ | 48 |
| Nordic anti-ageing skincare | 36 |
| Women's intimate-health probiotic | 10 |
| Italian men's face & neck treatment | 3 |

Five markets. The Nordic skincare brand runs it as its **second, third and
fourth biggest pitches** — three variants of the same study-led piece, at 22, 10
and 4 ads. When a brand builds three versions of one argument, it has usually
found something.

---

## The shape

The source of the reveal can be a study or a professional, and both versions
follow the same beats.

**Headline** — attributes the reveal to an authority, and states that the whole
category has been wrong. Not "we discovered" — *they* discovered, or *a person
who works in this* is telling you.

**First line of body** — the finding, stated as a shock. Usually a null result
or a reversal: they tested the mainstream thing and it did nothing; the
treatment everyone recommends works by a mechanism nobody explains; nine out of
ten of the products in the shops make the problem worse.

**The scale detail** — how many people, how long, who ran it. Or for the
professional version: how long they have been doing the job and what they have
seen. This is the line that makes the reveal weigh something.

**The mechanism** — why the mainstream thing fails, explained with a physical
image a child could hold. The sweep's versions use exactly this kind of picture:
a net with holes too small for what you are trying to push through; a treatment
that switches off the machinery rather than fixing it; a product that only ever
sits on the surface. The image matters more than the accuracy of the vocabulary.

**The turn to product** — yours is built around the thing the finding exposed.
Short. Two lines at most.

**Close** — usually a read-more, because this shape is normally pointing at a
longer page, not a product page.

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
{AUTHORITY} : "{WE'VE BEEN DOING THIS WRONG FOR {N} YEARS}"
   — or —
The {PROBLEM} mistake {AUDIENCE} keeps making — from {A PROFESSIONAL
OF N YEARS}

THE FINDING:
{AUTHORITY} {TESTED / TRACKED} {N PEOPLE} using {THE MAINSTREAM
SOLUTION} for {DURATION}. The result? {THE NULL OR THE REVERSAL}.

THE QUOTE:
"{WE EXPECTED SOMETHING SMALL. WE FOUND NOTHING.}" — {ROLE, NOT A
CELEBRITY}

THE CULPRIT, NAMED:
The reason is {ONE TECHNICAL-SOUNDING TERM, then immediately
translated}.

THE PICTURE:
Think of {THE BODY PART} like {AN EVERYDAY OBJECT}. {WHAT GETS
THROUGH}. {WHAT NEVER COULD}.

THE TURN:
Which is why {YOUR PRODUCT} is built {THE OTHER WAY} — {ONE LINE}.

CLOSE:
{SEE WHY / READ WHAT THEY FOUND} 👇
```

## Why it works

The reader has usually tried the mainstream solution, or feels guilty for not
trying it. Either way it sits above them as the thing they are *supposed* to do.
You cannot beat that with a claim of your own, because your claim is just a
company talking.

This shape does not argue with the mainstream solution. It brings someone else
to do it — someone with more standing than the brand and more standing than the
reader — and lets them say it. The brand is then merely the one relaying the
news.

The physical picture is the part that converts. Once a reader can *see* why the
usual thing cannot work — a thing too big to fit through a hole, a switch turned
off rather than a repair made — the argument stops being something they have to
take on faith and becomes something they can check against their own experience.
And it always checks out, because the thing genuinely did not work for them.

**The professional version is easier and safer to build than the study version.**
A named study with a named researcher and a participant count is a factual claim,
and if you write one you must be able to point at it. A twenty-year practitioner
describing what they see across a career is a first-person account, which is
both more defensible and often more persuasive — a working person saying what
their colleagues will not say out loud is a very strong opening.

If you go the study route, use a real one and describe it accurately. An
invented study is a legal and reputational problem, not a creative choice, and it
is not what this shape needs in order to work.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Wrenfield — a split ergonomic keyboard.**

> **A hand therapist of 18 years: "we're treating the wrong joint"**
>
> I've spent eighteen years treating office workers for wrist pain, and I'll say
> something most of my colleagues won't. The wrist brace almost never fixes it.
> It makes the week better and the year worse.
>
> In a normal caseload I'll see forty people a year who've worn one daily for
> months and come in no better than when they started. Not slightly better.
> The same.
>
> Here's why, and once you see it you can't unsee it. The pain is in the wrist
> but the cause is almost always at the shoulder. A standard keyboard forces
> your hands closer together than your shoulders are wide, so your forearms
> rotate inward all day to meet in the middle.
>
> Think of your arm like a rope under tension. You can splint the last few
> inches of it all you like. If the tension is coming from the other end, the
> splint just moves the strain somewhere quieter.
>
> That's the whole reason Wrenfield is two halves. You put them where your
> shoulders already are, and the rotation stops being necessary.
>
> See what changes in the first fortnight 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A person in their fifties in a plain consulting room, seated, mid-sentence,
> talking to someone just off camera rather than to the lens. Soft window light,
> neutral walls, a desk with actual working clutter on it. Professional but not
> corporate — no white coat, no stock-photo smile. Shot slightly off-centre with
> a static camera on a tripod. Documentary interview framing. Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Thirty to forty seconds. This shape needs the full explanation or it does not
convert, and it holds attention because the viewer is being told a secret.

- **0–3s:** they are already mid-sentence. The headline claim is the first thing
  out of their mouth. Text version on screen simultaneously.
- **3–10s:** the finding, delivered straight to camera-adjacent, static shot, no
  cuts. Stillness reads as confidence here; a moving camera reads as production.
- **10–14s:** the scale detail, with the numbers stamped on screen as they are
  said. This is the only place in the film where type should be emphatic.
- **14–26s:** the physical picture, as an animation. Twelve full seconds, which
  will feel long while you are editing it and is correct. Show the everyday object
  first on its own, then the body part mapped onto it, then the failure happening
  slowly. If you rush this section the whole ad stops working.
- **26–32s:** back to the person for the turn to product. Two lines, no more, and
  they should sound slightly reluctant to mention a brand at all.
- **32–38s:** read-more close. This shape usually leads to a page, not a cart, so
  the close should promise reading rather than buying.

Variants: change the professional, the room, the caseload number and the everyday
object in the picture. Keep the beat order. The animation is expensive to make
once and free to re-use across every version.
