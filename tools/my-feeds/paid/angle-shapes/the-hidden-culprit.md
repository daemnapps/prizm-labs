---

## The shape

**Headline** — a flat statement that contradicts something the reader believes,
or a benefit stated as an impossibility ("X can't do this. Y can.").

**First line of body** — describes the reader's failed loop in their own words.
The specific, slightly humiliating routine they are stuck in. Not the problem —
the *loop*. They try, it fails, they try again tomorrow.

**The turn — and this is the shape** — one short sentence that takes the blame
off the reader and puts it on something else. It is almost always two clauses:
*that is not X. That is Y.* The brands here run it as "you're not weak, you're
poisoned", "chocolate was never the problem, the sugar was", "your skin isn't
broken", "it's not motivation or willpower", "that's not normal ageing".

**Proof** — the mechanism of the culprit, in two or three plain sentences. What
the culprit is actually doing, physically, to cause the thing they blamed
themselves for. It has to be explainable to a twelve-year-old in one breath. The
sweep's examples: something binds to something and chokes it; the molecules are
too big to go in; the product sits on the surface and never reaches the layer
that matters.

**Close** — your product, named as the thing that goes at the culprit. Offer
optional, and in these ads it is usually a single line at the end.

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
{THE THING THEY TRUST} can't {DO THE JOB}. {YOUR THING} can.
   — or —
{FLAT CONTRADICTION OF WHAT THEY BELIEVE}

OPENING (the loop):
{WHAT THEY DO}. {WHAT HAPPENS}. {HOW THEY FEEL AN HOUR/DAY LATER}.
Then they do the whole thing again tomorrow.

THE TURN:
That's not a {WHAT THEY BLAME} problem. That's a {CULPRIT} problem.

MECHANISM (2–3 sentences, plain):
{CULPRIT} {PHYSICALLY DOES WHAT} to {WHAT PART OF THEM}. That's why
{EVERY PREVIOUS ATTEMPT} was always going to fail — {WHY IT COULDN'T REACH}.

RELIEF:
You weren't {DOING IT WRONG / LAZY / UNLUCKY}. You were aiming at the
wrong thing.

CLOSE:
{PRODUCT} goes at {CULPRIT} directly. {OFFER} 👇
```

## Why it works

A person who has had a problem for years has almost always built a private
theory about it, and the theory is usually some version of *it is my fault*. I'm
lazy. I don't stick at things. My genes. I got old. I didn't look after myself.

That belief is doing two things, and both are bad for you as the seller. It
makes them feel awful, and it makes them sceptical — because if the problem is
*them*, then no product can fix it, so why bother reading your ad.

This shape takes the accusation away and hands it to something external, which
does three jobs at once:

1. **Relief.** Being told it was never your fault is a genuinely pleasant thing
   to hear, and people keep reading things that feel good.
2. **It explains every past failure in one stroke.** They tried six products and
   none worked. If the culprit is something none of those six addressed, then six
   failures is no longer evidence that nothing works — it is evidence for your
   argument. You have converted their entire history of disappointment into proof.
3. **It makes your product the only logical next move.** Not the best of many.
   The only one pointed at the actual cause.

The failure mode is vagueness. "Toxins", "inflammation", "your lifestyle" — a
culprit that broad does not relieve anyone, because they cannot picture it and
cannot picture you beating it. Name one thing. Say what it physically does.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Northrow — a daily gut-health tea.**

> **You don't have a slow metabolism. You have a slow morning.**
>
> You eat well. You've cut things out. You've done the whole thing where you
> drink water first and eat later. And most days by eleven you're bloated,
> heavy, and quietly annoyed at your own body. Then you get up tomorrow and do
> it again.
>
> That's not a willpower problem. That's a transit-time problem.
>
> Food moving too slowly through you ferments before it finishes the journey.
> Fermenting food makes gas, gas makes pressure, pressure is what you're feeling
> at eleven o'clock. It has nothing to do with how much you ate or how good you
> were — it's about how long it sat there. Which is also why cutting more foods
> out never fixed it. You were changing what went in. The problem was the speed.
>
> You weren't undisciplined. You were aiming at the wrong end of it.
>
> Northrow is a morning tea built to move things along — bitter herbs that get
> the system started instead of fibre that adds to the queue. First cup within
> twenty minutes of waking. 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A woman in her thirties standing at a kitchen counter in the late morning, one
> hand resting flat on her stomach, looking down and slightly away. Ordinary
> home, real clutter, a half-finished cup beside her. Flat overcast daylight
> through the window — no golden hour, no styling. She looks tired rather than
> unwell. Documentary photograph, natural colour, slight grain. Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Twenty-five to thirty seconds. This shape needs more room than most because the
mechanism has to actually land.

- **0–4s:** the loop, as three fast cuts of the same person doing the same
  sensible thing on three different mornings. Same framing each time, tiny
  variations. The repetition is the point — the viewer should feel the groove
  before you name it.
- **4–6s:** freeze on the third frame. Colour drains slightly. The turn line
  lands on screen and in voice at the same moment: *that's not a ___ problem.*
  Full stop. Then a beat of silence before the second half of the line. The pause
  between the two clauses is the most important half-second in the film.
- **6–16s:** the mechanism, as a simple animated diagram. Keep it abstract and
  keep it crude — shapes and a line, not anatomy. Whatever the culprit physically
  does, show it doing that one thing, slowly, twice.
- **16–20s:** cut back to her. The relief line. She's not transformed, she's just
  listening. Let her face be neutral; a grateful reaction here reads as acting.
- **20–26s:** product, what it does, said once.
- **26–30s:** offer card.

To build variants: change the person and the loop entirely — a different age, a
different room, a different three mornings. Keep the turn line word-for-word and
keep the diagram identical across every version. Those two are the ad.
