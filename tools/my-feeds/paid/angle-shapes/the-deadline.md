# The deadline

**172 ads across 7 of the 9 brands — the most widespread shape in the sweep.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |

Seven brands out of nine. No other shape reaches that many markets. It is also
the shape with the *smallest* average commitment — most brands keep it under 30
ads. That combination is the lesson: **everybody runs it, nobody bets the
account on it.**

---

## The shape

There are three versions, and they are not interchangeable.

**Version A — the dated sale.** Headline is the code or the number. Body gives
exact dates, an exact discount, and one line on what the product is. Nothing
else. The two brands running the biggest versions both put the actual calendar
dates in.

**Version B — the stock warning.** Headline is an alarm. Body says demand
outran supply, gives a number of units left or a production delay in weeks, and
tells you what the wait will be if you miss it. The specificity of the
number is the entire mechanism.

**Version C — the honest shortage.** This is the interesting one. Headline
announces being out of stock, and the body explains *why* — a harvest ended, a
small supplier stopped, they will not substitute an ingredient to keep shelves
full. It converts a failure into a proof of standards. The Italian brand runs
this and closes by asking the reader to wait.

**Close** — in all three: buy now, or a price. Version C sometimes asks for
nothing at all, which is why it works.

```

BODY:
{EXACT WINDOW: day, date, to day, date}
{N}% off {SCOPE — sitewide, this range}
{ONE LINE ON WHAT THE PRODUCT IS}
{WHY STOCKING UP IS THE OBVIOUS MOVE}

VERSION B — THE STOCK WARNING
HEADLINE: {ALARM — low stock, final N units}
BODY:
{THE SPECIFIC NUMBER LEFT — never "limited"}
{WHY: demand outran production by N weeks}
{WHAT MISSING IT COSTS — a waitlist of N weeks}
{ONE LINE ON THE PRODUCT}

VERSION C — THE HONEST SHORTAGE
HEADLINE: {PRODUCT} is out of stock 😱
BODY:
We get {INGREDIENT} from {SPECIFIC SMALL SOURCE}. When {SEASONAL

SHORTCUT}. We wait.
That's why we run out. It's also why {N} customers trust it —
{WHAT NEVER CHANGES}.
CLOSE: In stock? {TRY IT, GUARANTEE}. Sold out? I'll ask you to wait.
```

## Why it works

Not because urgency is magic. It works because of what it does to a person who
has **already decided and not acted** — and in most categories that is the
biggest single group of people who will ever see your ad. They read something of
yours last week, thought "yes, probably", and then did nothing, because nothing
was happening today.

A deadline gives today a reason to be different from tomorrow. That is the whole
function, and it explains the two rules that decide whether it works:

1. **It only works on people you have already persuaded.** A deadline is not an
   argument. Run it alone at a cold stranger and it is just noise. Run it behind
   an argument they already saw, and it converts.
2. **The number has to be real and it has to be odd.** "Final 287 bottles" works
   in a way "limited stock" does not, because 287 sounds counted and "limited"
   sounds written. Specific dates work the same way. Round numbers read as
   marketing; odd numbers read as inventory.

**Version C deserves its own note.** It is the only one that survives repetition.
Versions A and B wear out fast — a brand permanently running out of stock is
obviously never running out of stock, and readers clock that within a couple of
exposures. Version C does not decay the same way, because the *reason* for the
shortage is a standards claim, and standards claims get stronger with repetition
rather than weaker. If you are only going to build one, build that one.

Keep the honesty real, though. If you say the harvest ended, the harvest has to
have ended.

## Worked example — Version C

**Made-up brand: Oakenshaw — a cast-iron seasoning oil.**

> **Oakenshaw is sold out again 😩**
>
> The oil is pressed from one orchard's worth of late-season nuts in Somerset.
> When that pressing is done for the year, it's done. There isn't a second
> supplier.
>
> We don't top it up with refined oil to make the batch bigger. We don't
> reformulate in a lean year. We wait for the next pressing.
>
> That's why we run out every autumn. It's also why 12,000 people keep a tin of
> this instead of the supermarket stuff — because what's in the tin this year is
> what was in it last year.
>
> In stock right now? Take it, and send it back within 60 days if it isn't
> better than what you're using.
>
> Sold out? Then I'm going to ask you to wait, which I know is annoying.

No discount, no countdown, no alarm. It is a scarcity ad that never asks for
urgency and gets it anyway.

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A dimly lit small workshop or store room at the end of a working day. Mostly
> empty wooden shelving with two or three products left on it, warm low light
> from one overhead bulb, dust visible in the air. A pair of hands in frame
> wrapping the last item in paper. Unglamorous, real, slightly melancholy.
> Documentary photograph, warm tungsten colour, grain. Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Fifteen to twenty seconds for versions A and B; twenty-five to thirty for
version C, which needs room to explain itself.

- **Versions A and B:** speed is the message. Hard cuts every second, the number
  on screen large and early, a visible counter or a date range stamped in a
  corner and left there the whole time. Do not get artful. This is a poster.
- **Version C:** the opposite. Slow handheld. Open on the empty shelf and hold
  for three full seconds before any words — the emptiness *is* the headline.
  Then one continuous move along the shelf while the reason is spoken, plainly,
  by someone who sounds like they actually own the business. No music or a single
  sustained note. At around 20s, cut to the source itself — the orchard, the
  supplier, the place — for one shot and one shot only. Close on the two-line
  ask: take it, or wait. No offer card, no logo animation. Let it end abruptly.

For variants of A and B: change the number and the date and re-cut. For version
C: change the shot of the source and the voice, keep the reasoning
word-for-word — it is a position statement, and positions should not paraphrase
themselves.
