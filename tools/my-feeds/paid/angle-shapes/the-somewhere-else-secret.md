Five markets. The foundation brand runs a single 101-ad pitch on it. And the
Italian brand is worth studying closely, because it runs this shape *in two
directions at once* — telling an American audience that Italians know something,
and telling an Italian audience that Americans envy them. Same argument, two
readers, both flattered.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — names the place, or the rank the product holds there. Number one
in a country. What women in a particular market use. The oldest way of doing
this.

**First line of body** — states the gap plainly: over there, they do it
differently, and it shows. Often as an observation rather than a claim — people
keep asking me why, or foreigners spend fortunes trying to get what we have.

**The reason the place is different** — and this is the part that separates a
working version from a weak one. It cannot be "they just have better taste." It
needs a cause: a climate that forces plants to concentrate their defences, a
market so competitive that only the good survive, an ingredient that only grows
in one season in one valley, a tradition that has had four hundred years to get
the mistakes out.

**Proof** — a rank, a rating, a length of tradition, or a named region. One line.

**Close** — now available here. The offer is that the reader gets access to
something that was previously local.

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
The #1 {CATEGORY} in {COUNTRY} 👉
   — or —
Why {AUDIENCE} are choosing {THIS} over {THE EXPENSIVE LOCAL NORM}

THE GAP:
In {PLACE} — {WHY THAT PLACE HAS STANDING IN THIS CATEGORY} — they
{DO IT DIFFERENTLY}, not {THE WAY WE DO IT HERE}.

THE REASON THE PLACE IS DIFFERENT (this is the load-bearing part):
{THE CLIMATE / THE COMPETITION / THE SEASON / THE TRADITION} means
{WHAT THAT FORCES}. {WHY THAT MAKES THE RESULT BETTER, PHYSICALLY}.

THE OBSERVATION:
{PEOPLE KEEP ASKING ME WHY} / {THEY SPEND FORTUNES TRYING TO GET
WHAT WE JUST HAVE}.

PROOF:
{RANK / RATING / YEARS / NAMED REGION}.

CLOSE:
Now here. {OFFER} 👇
```

## Why it works

Every market has a reputation, and the reader already holds those reputations
whether or not they could defend them. Germany makes things properly. Japan is
precise. France knows food. Italy knows how to look good. Korea is ahead on
skincare. Scandinavia is clean and serious.

You did not build that belief, you do not have to prove it, and it costs you
nothing to borrow. It is the cheapest credibility available to an advertiser,
which is why five different brands in this sweep are doing it at once.

It does three things:

1. **It explains why the reader has not heard of you** — you were not here, you
   were there — which turns your obscurity from a weakness into the premise.
2. **It relieves them of a failure.** Everything they tried was local, and local
   was working from a worse starting point. Again, not their fault.
3. **It flatters.** Reading it makes the reader someone in the know rather than
   someone being sold to.

**The one rule: give the place a mechanism.** A country name on its own is
decoration and readers are entirely used to ignoring it. A country name attached
to a physical reason is an argument. Plants under constant cold stress make more
of the compounds that protect them, so an extract from there is more
concentrated — that is a mechanism, and the reader can carry it away. "Nordic
botanicals" alone is a label on a bottle.

Also: do not stack it with other borrowed authorities. A place, plus a study,
plus a celebrity, plus a tradition reads as someone reaching. Pick the place and
commit.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Fjellvik — a wool base layer.**

> **The base layer Norwegian fishermen have worn for 60 years**
>
> There is a reason nobody in northern Norway wears the technical synthetic kit
> that fills the shops here, and it isn't tradition for its own sake.
>
> It's that synthetics stop working the moment you stop moving. They're built for
> people generating heat — runners, cyclists, people on their way somewhere.
> Stand still on a wet deck for nine hours at two degrees and a synthetic layer
> holds your sweat against you and takes your warmth with it.
>
> Wool from sheep bred for that coast has a longer, coarser fibre than the
> merino most base layers use. It traps more air, and — this is the part that
> matters — it keeps insulating when it's damp, because the fibre holds water
> inside itself rather than between the threads. Which is why it's what you wear
> when you can't leave and you can't move.
>
> People keep asking why we don't make a lighter version. We can't. The fibre is
> the reason it works.
>
> Spun in Tromsø, same mill since 1961.
>
> Now shipping here for the first time. 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A cold northern coastal working scene at first light — grey-blue water, a
> weathered wooden jetty, low cloud on a hillside behind. One person in heavy
> working clothes at the far side of frame, small against the landscape, not
> looking at camera. Desaturated, blue-grey palette with one small point of warm
> colour. Wide, still, cold. Photographic, natural light, documentary.
> Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Twenty-five to thirty seconds. The place has to be *felt* before the argument
arrives, so front-load atmosphere and back-load the reasoning.

- **0–5s:** the place, wide, almost no movement. Ambient sound only — wind,
  water, a rope. No voice, no music, no text for the first three seconds. In a
  feed of noise, five seconds of cold silence is the hook.
- **5–8s:** headline text arrives, plain, over the landscape. Still no voice.
- **8–18s:** the mechanism, in close-up on the material itself. Macro shots: the
  fibre, the weave, water on it, water inside it. Voice starts here and explains
  the physical reason. Close-ups are essential — the argument is about a material
  property, so the viewer needs to see the material.
- **18–22s:** one shot of the person working, still not addressing camera. The
  proof line (the mill, the year, the region) as type over this shot.
- **22–26s:** cut, for the first time, to somewhere that looks like the viewer's
  own life — an ordinary street, an ordinary morning, the same garment. This
  transition is the entire close: *the thing from there, here.*
- **26–30s:** offer.

Variants: re-shoot the opening landscape and the closing here-shot; keep the
macro material section and the mechanism voiceover unchanged. The macro footage
is the expensive part and it never needs to be made twice.
