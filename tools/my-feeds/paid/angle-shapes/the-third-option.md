# The third option

**969 ads across 5 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Men's hair-thinning shampoo | 942 |
| Men's testosterone supplement | 17 |
| Garlic & oregano-oil supplements | 4 |
| Nordic anti-ageing skincare | 3 |
| Women's intimate-health probiotic | 3 |

This is the shape carrying the single largest pitch in the whole sweep: **835
ads** on one argument at the shampoo brand, plus two more variants of the same
structure at 85 and 22. Nearly half that brand's entire account is this one
move.

It also shows up in four other markets that have nothing to do with hair — which
is the point of thinking in shapes.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — usually a crowd number or a flat refusal. Either "this many
people switched" or "no needles, no pills, no prescription". The headline's job
is to signal *I know what you were about to say*.

**First line of body** — names the two options the reader already knows about,
by name, without softening them. Not vague alternatives — the actual two things
they have been circling for months.

**Then: disqualify both, on a specific cost.** This is the whole engine. Not
"they don't work" — a price tag, a side effect, a needle, a dependency, a
commitment. Something concrete enough that the reader has already privately
worried about it. The ads in this sweep disqualify with: side effects, needles
into the scalp, a four-thousand-dollar-a-year bill, hormonal interference,
lifelong dependency, and products that burn.

**Proof** — the mechanism, in one sentence: your thing goes after the same
target as the two rejected options, by a different route. This is essential.
Without it the reader assumes the third option is just the weak option.

**Close** — the low-commitment ask. A price, a long guarantee, "no prescription
needed". The close should feel like the *small* choice, because the reader has
just been shown two big ones.

---

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
{NUMBER} people made the switch
   — or —
No {REJECTED OPTION A}. No {REJECTED OPTION B}. No {THE THING THEY FEAR}.

OPENING LINE:
Every way of dealing with {PROBLEM} either asks you to {COST OF OPTION A}
or {COST OF OPTION B}.

TURN:
{PRODUCT} does neither.

MECHANISM:
It goes after {THE REAL TARGET — the same one A and B target} using
{YOUR ROUTE}. {ONE LINE ON WHY THAT ROUTE AVOIDS BOTH COSTS}.

EFFORT:
{HOW LITTLE THE PERSON HAS TO DO — time per day, times per week}.

CLOSE:
{OFFER} — {GUARANTEE IN DAYS}. 👇
```

## Why it works

Almost nobody arrives at an ad as a blank page. If they have the problem, they
have already looked it up, and they already know the two mainstream answers. And
they have not taken either one — because both scared them, or cost too much, or
demanded something they did not want to give.

So they are sitting in a specific, uncomfortable place: they know what they are
"supposed" to do, and they are not doing it, and they feel a bit bad about that.

This shape walks straight into that room and says the quiet part: *those two
options are genuinely a lot to ask, and you were right to hesitate.* It removes
the guilt before it makes an offer. That is why the crowd number in the headline
works so hard — it tells the reader that hesitating was not weakness, because
eighteen thousand other people hesitated too and found a different door.

The mechanism line is the part beginners skip, and skipping it kills the ad. If
you reject the two strong options and do not explain how yours reaches the same
target, you have just described something weaker than what they already turned
down.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Pellow — a cooling mattress topper.**

> **31,000 people stopped buying a new mattress**
>
> Every fix for waking up hot at 3am either asks you to spend two thousand
> pounds on a new mattress, or take something to knock yourself out through it.
>
> Pellow does neither.
>
> The reason you wake at 3am isn't the mattress — it's heat trapped between you
> and it. Your body needs to drop about a degree to stay in deep sleep, and foam
> holds that degree right where you're lying. Pellow is a topper with an open
> airflow core that moves the heat out instead of storing it. Same problem
> everyone else is throwing money at, solved from the layer that's actually
> causing it.
>
> It goes on top of the bed you already own. Two minutes, once.
>
> 40% off this week, and 100 nights to send it back. 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A dimly lit bedroom at night, cool blue moonlight through a half-open blind,
> the striped shadow falling across rumpled white bedding. A bedside clock face
> visible but slightly out of focus reading just past three. No person, or at most
> a suggestion of a shoulder at the very edge of frame. Quiet, still,
> uncomfortable. Photographic, documentary feel, slight grain, not glossy.
> Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Twenty to twenty-five seconds. The structure of the film should mirror the
structure of the argument: two doors close, one opens.

- **0–3s:** the dark bedroom, almost no motion — just the faintest handheld
  drift. Headline in plain white type. The stillness is the hook; everything else
  in the feed is moving.
- **3–9s:** the two rejected options, one after the other, as two hard cuts. Show
  them as objects, not people — a price tag, a blister pack, a receipt. Each gets
  about three seconds and a slam cut, no transition. Fast and cold.
- **9–11s:** a beat of black. One second. This is the hinge and it needs the
  empty space.
- **11–18s:** light comes up. The mechanism in one simple moving diagram — heat
  drawn as warm colour moving out and away rather than pooling. Keep it crude and
  legible; a beginner-clear diagram beats a beautiful one nobody parses.
- **18–22s:** the product in a bright morning version of the same bedroom. Same
  camera position as the opening shot, now warm and daylit. That match is the
  whole payoff — let it land for a full second before the offer card.
- **22–25s:** offer and guarantee.

To build variants: change the two rejected options, change the room, change the
voice. Keep the dark-room-to-light-room match cut. That is the bit doing the
work.
