# The moment it got noticed

**160 ads across 3 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Italian men's face & neck treatment | 80 |
| Men's hair-thinning shampoo | 70 |
| Women's intimate-health probiotic | 10 |

Three brands, and all three sell something that is **visible or noticeable to
other people**. That is the boundary of this shape — it does not work on a
private problem, and it works extremely well on a public one. The Italian
brand's second-biggest pitch, 80 ads, is built entirely on which body part
gives a man's age away.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — either names the tell ("the thing that gives you away is not
what you think") or is a plain crowd-count line with the scene doing all the
work underneath.

**First line of body** — a specific scene with another person in it. A
photograph at a wedding. Someone at the gym pulling you aside. A partner saying
something and then not finishing the sentence. It is always a moment where the
problem stopped being private.

**The detail that makes it real** — one small, awkward, too-specific fact. The
sun on the top of the head. The way he stopped taking his hat off. Sitting where
the light was dim in photographs. Nobody invents details like that, which is
exactly why they are believed.

**The turn** — the realisation that it had been visible for a while before the
moment. They already knew and were managing around it. The moment did not create
the problem; it ended the ability to ignore it.

**Proof** — brief. Usually either what the thing actually is physically, or a
crowd number. This shape does not need much proof because the scene has already
done the persuading.

**Close** — straightforward. Often a discount and a long guarantee.

---

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
The thing that gives away {WHAT THEY'RE HIDING} isn't {WHAT THEY
ASSUME}. It's {THE ACTUAL TELL}.
   — or —
{CROWD NUMBER} made the switch ⭐️

THE SCENE:
{ORDINARY EVENT — a wedding, a gym, the end of a normal day}.
{WHAT SOMEONE ELSE DID OR SAID}. {WHAT THEY DIDN'T SAY}.

THE DETAIL:
{ONE TOO-SPECIFIC PHYSICAL FACT — a light source, a camera angle, a
piece of clothing, a change in someone's behaviour}

THE QUIET ADMISSION:
{HOW LONG THEY'D BEEN MANAGING AROUND IT}. {THE STORY THEY'D BEEN
TELLING THEMSELVES — it's the lighting, it's just wet, it's normal}.

THE END OF DENIAL:
Not bad enough to call it a disaster. Just bad enough that
{THE AVOIDANCE BEHAVIOUR} wasn't going to work much longer.

PROOF (short):
{WHAT'S PHYSICALLY HAPPENING, ONE LINE} / {CROWD NUMBER}

CLOSE:
{OFFER} — {LONG GUARANTEE} 👇
```

## Why it works

Everybody with a visible slow-moving problem has a private managing system, and
they do not think of it as a system. They have simply stopped doing a few
things. They pick their seat. They avoid a certain light. They don't take the
hat off. They shower before the gym now. None of it was a decision, and all of
it is invisible to them until someone names it.

This shape names it. And the specific sensation of being seen — *how do they
know I do that* — is one of the very few things that can stop a scroll without
any promise, discount or claim.

Then it does something clever with guilt. It does not say you should have acted
sooner. It says the moment arrived on its own and nobody could have stopped it.
The reader is not at fault for waiting; they simply hit the point where waiting
ran out. That keeps them reading instead of closing the ad in irritation.

**Two rules, and both matter.**

First, **the scene has to have another person in it.** The whole shape depends
on the problem becoming social. A person alone noticing their own problem is
just a worry; a person realising someone else noticed is an event.

Second, **do not add shame.** The sweep's examples are careful about this — in
the strongest versions the other person is gentle, or says nothing at all, or
the narrator only realises later. An ad that makes the reader feel humiliated
gets closed. An ad that makes them feel *seen* gets read to the end. The
distance between those two is entirely in how kindly the other person in the
scene behaves.

This is also the shape with the highest risk of being unpleasant if you get it
wrong, so it is worth writing carefully and reading back once as the person it is
about.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Thistledown — a snoring mouthpiece.**

> **The thing that tells you it's bad isn't the noise. It's where she's
> sleeping.**
>
> It wasn't a row. There was no conversation about it at all. She just started
> going up at ten, and I'd come up at midnight, and after a while the spare room
> door was shut when I got there. Neither of us ever said the word.
>
> Here's the detail I keep coming back to: she'd made the bed in there. Properly.
> Not thrown a duvet on it — made it, with the pillows from the cupboard. That's
> not a bad night. That's a decision somebody made a while ago.
>
> And if I'm honest I'd been managing it for two years before that. Falling
> asleep on my side on purpose. Going up first so she was already under. Telling
> myself it was only when I'd had a drink.
>
> It was never bad enough to call it a problem. It was just bad enough that my
> workarounds had quietly stopped working.
>
> What's actually happening is the tongue falling back and closing the airway
> when the jaw drops. Thistledown holds the jaw a few millimetres forward so it
> can't. That's the whole mechanism.
>
> 40% off and 90 nights to decide. 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A hallway at night, seen from partway along it. A closed bedroom door at the
> end with a thin line of warm light underneath it, and a second door standing
> open to a dark spare room with a neatly made bed just visible. Nobody in shot.
> Cool blue darkness against the one warm line of light. Still, quiet, loaded.
> Photographic, natural low light, slight grain. Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Twenty-five to thirty-five seconds. This shape lives on restraint. Every instinct
to add coverage makes it worse.

- **0–4s:** the scene, and only the scene. No voice yet. Slow handheld drift
  toward the closed door. Four seconds of a corridor is a long time in a feed and
  that is exactly why it stops people.
- **4–8s:** voice starts, mid-thought, quiet. Headline text on screen at the
  same time.
- **8–14s:** the too-specific detail, shown. In this example: the made bed, one
  slow pan across it, the pillows. One shot. Do not cut to a reaction face — the
  object carries more than an actor can.
- **14–20s:** the quiet admission, over ordinary footage of the workarounds. Show
  the behaviour, not the emotion: going up first, turning onto one side. Flat,
  undramatic, no music swell.
- **20–24s:** the end-of-denial line, over black or over a held still frame.
- **24–30s:** the mechanism, as one simple diagram — five seconds, crude, clear.
  The tonal shift from a dark hallway to a plain explanatory graphic is a relief
  for the viewer and it should feel like one.
- **30–34s:** offer and guarantee, plainly.

Variants: change the scene entirely — the photograph, the gym, the pulled-aside
conversation — and keep the mechanism section and the offer card identical. The
scene is what you re-shoot fifty times; the last ten seconds never change.
