# The spec sheet

**The biggest shape in the sweep. 1,558 ads across 6 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Colour-adapting foundation, women 45+ | 1,032 |
| Garlic & oregano-oil supplements | 462 |
| Men's testosterone supplement | 47 |
| Teen acne skincare | 11 |
| Nordic anti-ageing skincare | 3 |
| No-added-sugar chocolate | 3 |

One in four ads in the entire sweep is this shape. The foundation brand's single
biggest pitch — **924 ads on its own** — is a spec sheet. The supplement brand's
biggest — 313 ads — is a spec sheet. It is not fancy and it is not clever, and
it is what the money is on.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — names the product or its single most unusual attribute. No
mystery, no tease. The reader knows within four words what is being sold.

**First line of body** — one short frustration the reader already recognises, or
a direct question that sorts qualified people in. Not a story. One line, then
move.

**Proof** — a vertical list of ticked attributes. Five to eight of them. Each is
one line, each is a fact rather than a feeling: what is in it, what it is free
of, who tested it, how many people bought it, what the guarantee is.

**Close** — a tap. "See how it works", "Shop now", a price, a discount. The
reader is being sent to buy, immediately.

There is no arc here at all. It is a shelf label with a discount on it, and the
reason it wins is that it does not pretend to be anything else.

---

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
{THE ONE UNUSUAL THING ABOUT THIS PRODUCT, STATED FLAT}

OPENING LINE:
{THE FRUSTRATION THEY HAVE PUT UP WITH FOR YEARS}, there's finally another way.
   — or —
{DIRECT QUESTION THAT ONLY YOUR BUYER SAYS YES TO}?

BODY:
Introducing {PRODUCT} — {WHAT IT IS IN SIX WORDS}.

✅ {WHAT IT DOES, BENEFIT 1}
✅ {WHAT IT DOES, BENEFIT 2}
✅ {WHAT IT DOES, BENEFIT 3}
✅ {THE FORMAT / INGREDIENT SPEC THAT MAKES IT CREDIBLE}
✅ {THE TESTING OR SOURCING FACT}
✅ {THE SCALE FACT — orders shipped, customers, rating}
✅ {THE GUARANTEE, IN DAYS}

CLOSE:
{OFFER — price, or percentage off} — {CTA VERB} 👇
```

Six to eight ticks. Under six it looks thin; over eight nobody reads to the
bottom.

---

## Why it works

Most ads ask the reader to feel something before they know what is being sold.
That is a lot to ask from a stranger scrolling. The spec sheet asks for nothing
and gives everything.

Three things are doing the work:

1. **It qualifies instantly.** A person who does not want this leaves in half a
   second and costs you nothing. A person who does want it has their whole
   decision in front of them at once.
2. **Ticks read as facts.** The same sentence as prose reads as a claim; the
   same sentence with a tick in front reads as a spec. The reader checks them
   like a list rather than judging them like an argument.
3. **It answers the objection before it forms.** The testing line, the scale
   line and the guarantee line are each pre-empting a separate reason to say no,
   and they do it in three lines instead of three paragraphs.

It is also the cheapest shape to build a hundred versions of, which is exactly
why the two most concentrated accounts in the sweep lean on it.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Brightwell — a countertop water filter.**

> **The filter that shows you what it caught**
>
> You've been drinking from a jug filter you've never once looked inside.
> There's finally a version that doesn't hide it.
>
> Brightwell is a countertop filter with a clear capture chamber.
>
> ✅ Removes chlorine, lead and microplastics down to 0.5 microns
> ✅ Clear chamber — you can see what it pulled out of your water
> ✅ One cartridge lasts 6 months, not 6 weeks
> ✅ No plumbing, no drilling — it sits on the counter
> ✅ Independently lab-tested against NSF standards
> ✅ 41,000 households and a 4.8 rating
> ✅ 60-day money-back guarantee, no questions
>
> $89 instead of $149 this week. See how it works 👇

Note what is absent: no story, no villain, no journey. Seven facts and a price.

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> Clean overhead product shot on a pale grey stone kitchen counter, soft
> north-facing daylight from the left, a single tall glass of clear water beside
> a matte-white countertop appliance with a transparent chamber. Shallow depth of
> field, the glass sharp and the background falling off softly. Minimal props: one
> folded linen cloth, one sprig of green. No text, no hands, no people.
> Photographic, editorial, not rendered. Vertical 9:16, generous empty space in
> the top third for a headline.

**Motion note**

Fifteen to twenty seconds, and the movement is the list, not the camera.

- **0–2s:** static hero shot, headline types on in the empty top third. Hold.
- **2–12s:** the ticks arrive one at a time, roughly 1.2 seconds apart, each
  sliding up from below with a small settle. Old ticks stay on screen and stack —
  the reader should watch the list *accumulate*, because the pile is the argument.
  Camera does almost nothing: a slow 5% push-in across the whole stretch.
- **12–15s:** the stack holds for a full beat with nothing moving. Silence here
  is worth more than another effect.
- **15–18s:** price snaps in under the stack, old price with a strike through it,
  then the button.

Swap the voiceover, the counter, the daylight and the hand model to build the
next fifty versions. The list stays identical — that is the part that is working.
