# The switch story

**408 ads across 6 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Colour-adapting foundation, women 45+ | 318 |
| Men's hair-thinning shampoo | 43 |
| Teen acne skincare | 23 |
| Women's intimate-health probiotic | 12 |
| No-added-sugar chocolate | 7 |
| Nordic anti-ageing skincare | 5 |

Six markets. The foundation brand's second-biggest pitch is a switch story on
**303 ads** — a woman explaining that she'd given up on her category entirely at
a named age, and what changed.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — a line of surrender, in quote marks, with a specific number in
it. An age, a count of years, a number of products. The quotation marks matter:
they signal a person talking, not a brand selling, before the reader has read a
word.

**First line of body** — the list of what they tried, in concrete objects. Not
"I tried everything" — the actual drawer full of bottles, the three
specialists, the serum that cost a fortune, the prescription. Objects, because
objects are checkable and adjectives are not.

**The middle** — how each one failed, and specifically *how it failed*. Worked
for a week then came back. Looked fine for three hours then didn't. Couldn't
tell if it was doing anything at all. This is the part beginners rush and it is
the part that earns the belief.

**The resignation** — a line where they gave up and accepted it. This is
essential. Without it the story is an advert; with it the story has a floor, and
the reader who is currently sitting on that same floor recognises themselves.

**The turn** — how they came across the thing, told flatly and almost
reluctantly. Someone mentioned it. A friend used it. They nearly didn't bother.
The reluctance is doing real work: a person who was easy to convince is not a
useful witness.

**Proof** — what changed, told small. Not "transformed". One specific observable
thing: it didn't slide into the lines by noon, the shedding slowed, I stopped
checking in the mirror.

**Close** — soft. "This is the one I keep around." Offer, if any, comes after
the story is finished, never inside it.

---

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
"{LINE OF SURRENDER, WITH A NUMBER — an age, a count of years}"

WHAT I TRIED (objects, not adjectives):
{OBJECT 1}. {OBJECT 2}. {OBJECT 3}. {THE EXPENSIVE ONE THAT DID NOTHING}.

HOW EACH FAILED:
Every one of them {THE SPECIFIC FAILURE PATTERN — worked for a week,
looked fine for three hours, I couldn't tell}.

THE RESIGNATION:
I'd decided {THE THING} just wasn't for {PEOPLE LIKE ME} any more.

THE TURN:
Then {HOW THEY HEARD OF IT — casual, secondhand}. I almost didn't
{TRY IT / ORDER IT}.

WHAT CHANGED (small and specific):
{ONE OBSERVABLE THING, MEASURED IN HOURS OR WEEKS}.

CLOSE:
{QUIET RECOMMENDATION}. {OFFER, AFTER THE STORY ENDS} 👇
```

## Why it works

People do not trust brands and they do trust people, and everyone knows this, so
it is not the whole answer. The real reason this shape carries 300+ ads at a
brand that could run anything is more specific than that.

**It hands the reader a defensible reason to have failed so far.** Somebody just
told them, in detail, about six things that did not work. If they have also
failed at six things, they are now in company rather than alone, and they have a
third-party account of why those things fail. That is worth more than any claim
about your product.

**It survives scepticism better than a claim does.** A claim invites the reader
to argue with it. A story does not present anything to argue with — it just
reports. The reader can dislike the product and still finish the story, and
finishing is most of the battle.

**The reluctance in the turn is load-bearing.** "I almost didn't order it" is
doing more work than every superlative in the ad. It tells the reader the
narrator was not an easy mark, which means their eventual endorsement cost
something.

Two failure modes to avoid. First, results that are too big — "ten years
younger overnight" collapses the whole thing back into an advert. Small and
specific beats large and vague every time. Second, no resignation beat. If the
narrator never actually gave up, the reader on the floor does not see themselves
in it.

**A short variant worth knowing.** Three brands run a compressed version — no
struggle arc at all, just a person showing the thing and saying people keep
asking about it. Twenty words, casual, phone-shot. Under thirty ads across the
sweep, so a minor pattern, but it is cheap to make and it slots into the same
production as the long version.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Marden — anti-glare computer glasses.**

> **"I'd accepted that by four o'clock I just wouldn't be able to focus."**
>
> Three pairs of blue-light glasses off the internet. A screen filter that made
> everything look yellow. An eye test that came back fine. A £200 monitor
> someone swore would fix it.
>
> Every single one made no difference I could actually point to. The filter I
> took off after a week because I couldn't judge colour through it. The glasses I
> couldn't tell apart from not wearing glasses. The monitor was lovely and my
> head hurt at four o'clock anyway.
>
> So I stopped looking. I decided some people just get tired eyes and I was one
> of them, and I'd work around it.
>
> Then someone at work mentioned she'd got a pair made for the actual brightness
> range of a screen rather than a blue tint. I nearly didn't bother. I'd already
> bought three pairs of glasses that did nothing.
>
> What changed is small and I'll say it exactly: I stopped noticing four
> o'clock. Not "my vision transformed" — I just got to the end of a working day
> without the thing where you close one eye to read.
>
> That's it. It's the pair I keep at the desk now.
>
> 30 days to send them back if they don't do the same for you. 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A woman in her forties sitting at an ordinary home desk, talking directly to a
> phone propped in front of her, mid-sentence. Real room — a mug, a cable, a
> plant that needs water. Window light from the side, slightly uneven and a bit
> harsh on one cheek. She looks like she's explaining something to a friend, not
> presenting. Phone-camera quality, not cinematic. Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Thirty-five to fifty seconds. This is the one shape where length is an asset —
the credibility comes from the detail, and detail takes time.

- **0–3s:** she's already talking when the ad starts. No opening greeting, no
  logo, no build. Start mid-sentence on the surrender line. The feeling should be
  that the viewer joined something already in progress.
- **3–14s:** the list of failures. Cut away to the objects themselves — a drawer,
  three boxes, a receipt — as she names them. One cut per object, quick. These
  cutaways are what make the story checkable.
- **14–18s:** back to her face for the resignation line, and hold on it. No cuts
  here. Let it be slightly awkward. A pause that feels a second too long is worth
  more than a cutaway.
- **18–26s:** the turn, told at the same flat speed as everything else. Do not
  let the music lift here. The moment the score swells, it becomes an advert.
- **26–38s:** the small specific result. Show it if it is showable, in ordinary
  conditions — no ring light, no before/after split screen.
- **38–45s:** she finishes. *Then* the offer card, as a separate object after the
  story has clearly ended.

To build the next hundred: change the person completely — age, accent, room,
objects, the exact four o'clock detail. Keep the beat order and keep the
music flat until after the story ends.
