# The sorter

**228 ads across 2 of the 9 brands.**

| Market | Ads on this shape |
|---|---|
| Teen acne skincare | 202 |
| Garlic & oregano-oil supplements | 26 |

Two brands, so it is a narrower finding than the big shapes — but look at where
those 202 ads sit. The teen acne brand runs **162 pitches**, more than anyone
else in the sweep, and its top two pitches are both this shape, at 97 and 86
ads. Out of one hundred and sixty-two attempts, the two that pulled away are the
same structure. That is a meaningful result even from one brand.

---

## The shape

**Headline** — an instruction to understand or identify, not a promise. "Work
out which kind you have." It offers information, not a result, which is why it
does not read like an ad.

**First line of body** — a run of different symptoms across different days or
different places. Monday this, Tuesday that, Wednesday the other. Or: heavy,
puffy, sluggish, stuck. The reader is meant to recognise **more than one** of
them and think *yes, all of those, at the same time, that's exactly it.*

**The turn** — you do not have one problem behaving randomly. You have several
distinct things happening at once, and they have different causes. This is the
reveal, and it is a genuinely useful piece of information, which is why the
reader keeps going.

**The sort** — name the types. Three to five, each with a one-line description
specific enough that the reader can place themselves. This is the part that gets
saved, screenshotted and sent to a friend.

**Proof** — either they all share one root underneath (so one product covers
them), or your product happens to address the whole set. You have to close this
loop or you have just told someone they have four problems and given them no
way out.

**Close** — soft, and usually about finding out more rather than buying.

---

## The template

```
HEADLINE:
Work out which {PROBLEM} you actually have

OPENING (the run):
{DAY 1}: {SYMPTOM A, in a specific place}.
{DAY 2}: {SYMPTOM B, different in kind}.
{DAY 3}: {SYMPTOM C}.
   — or —
{ADJECTIVE}, {ADJECTIVE}, {ADJECTIVE}, {ADJECTIVE}?

THE TURN:
Your {BODY PART / SITUATION} isn't broken, and it isn't random.
You've got more than one thing going on at the same time.

THE SORT:
🔹 {TYPE 1} — caused by {CAUSE 1}
🔹 {TYPE 2} — caused by {CAUSE 2}
🔹 {TYPE 3} — caused by {CAUSE 3}
🔹 {TYPE 4} — caused by {CAUSE 4}

WHY ONE THING COVERS THEM:
{EITHER: all four run back to ONE root — name it.
 OR: your product addresses each of the four, listed against them.}

WHY EVERYTHING FAILED BEFORE:
Anything aimed at only {TYPE 1} was never going to touch {TYPES 2–4}.

CLOSE:
{FIND OUT WHICH ONES ARE YOURS} 👇
```

## Why it works

Somebody with a messy, multi-symptom problem has usually concluded that their
body is being erratic. That conclusion is miserable, and it is also
paralysing — you cannot act on randomness. So they alternate between products
without a theory, each one aimed at whichever symptom is loudest this week.

This shape replaces randomness with a map. Four things going on is a much better
place to stand than one thing behaving unpredictably, even though it sounds
worse on paper. It is actionable. It gives the reader a name for what they have.

Three specific mechanics:

1. **Self-identification is participation.** The reader is doing something while
   they read — checking themselves against a list — and a person who is
   participating does not scroll.
2. **It explains a history of failed purchases without insulting the buyer.**
   Every product they tried was aimed at one type. That is why it half-worked,
   then stopped. Nobody was stupid; the aim was just narrow.
3. **It earns a save.** A genuinely useful list gets screenshotted and passed on,
   which is free distribution an ad does not normally get.

The one thing you must not skip is the close of the loop. If you sort the reader
into four types and then do not explain why one product handles all four, you
have made their problem feel bigger and left them nowhere to go. In the sweep,
both brands close it the same way — everything runs back to one shared root
underneath.

## Worked example

**Made-up brand: Clovebank — a dog itch supplement.**

> **Work out which itch your dog actually has**
>
> Monday he's chewing his back paws. Tuesday it's the base of the tail. Thursday
> his ears are warm and he's shaking his head. Sunday he's fine and you assume
> it's over.
>
> Your dog isn't being dramatic and it isn't random. There are usually three or
> four different itches happening at once, and they don't come from the same
> place.
>
> 🔹 **Paws and belly** — almost always something he's walking on or lying on
> 🔹 **Base of the tail** — nearly always fleas, even when you can't find any
> 🔹 **Ears, with head-shaking** — yeast, which thrives when the skin barrier is
>   already struggling
> 🔹 **All over, worse in spring** — pollen, same as people
>
> Here's what ties them together. Every one of those four is his immune system
> over-reacting to something that shouldn't be a big deal — and how loudly it
> over-reacts depends on how well his gut is regulating it. That's the shared
> root. It's also why the anti-itch spray helped the paws for a week and did
> nothing for the ears.
>
> Clovebank works on that root rather than on one location.
>
> Find out which ones you're dealing with 👇

## Make it a video

**Image prompt**

> A clean flat-lay on a plain off-white surface, shot straight down. Four small
> labelled cards laid out in a two-by-two grid with generous space between them,
> each card a different soft colour. Soft even light, no hard shadows. Simple,
> calm, almost like a reference chart rather than an advert. Nothing glossy.
> Vertical 9:16.

**Motion note**

Twenty-five to thirty seconds. The film is a chart being filled in, and it
should feel like being taught something.

- **0–5s:** the symptom run, as fast real-footage cuts with the day or the place
  stamped on each. One second each, four of them. Deliberately scattered and
  slightly chaotic — the viewer should feel the mess before you organise it.
- **5–8s:** everything stops. Clean white. The turn line alone on screen, held
  for a beat longer than feels comfortable. The switch from chaos to stillness is
  the whole emotional move of this ad.
- **8–20s:** the four types arrive one at a time into a grid, each with its cause
  written beneath it. Three seconds each. Keep the typography plain and
  chart-like, not branded — the more it looks like a reference and the less it
  looks like an ad, the better it performs.
- **20–24s:** the four boxes slide together into one, and the shared root appears
  underneath. This is a single, simple visual move and it is the payoff of the
  entire film. Do not decorate it.
- **24–28s:** product, once, quietly. Soft close.

To build variants: change the symptom run entirely (different days, different
subject, different four cuts) and keep the grid section identical. The grid is
the asset; the opening is the disposable part.
