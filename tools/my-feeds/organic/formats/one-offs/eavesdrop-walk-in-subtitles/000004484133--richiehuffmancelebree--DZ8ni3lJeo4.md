**Why it works**

The pinned opening line is an offer of access, not a claim — "want to hear what X sounds like?" makes the next 13 seconds feel like something you were let in on rather than something you were sold. Because the camera walks in from outside and nobody ever looks at it or performs for it, the subtitled dialogue reads as real, and the room, the chandeliers and the printed agendas do the status work without a single word about them.

**What carries the value**

The overheard conversation. Real people caught mid-sentence with printed agendas in front of them, made readable by running subtitles — the persuasion is that you are eavesdropping on a routine that already exists, not watching one staged for you. Everything else (the house, the chandeliers, the documents) is set dressing that proves the routine is real.

**Shoot it**

- **Camera:** phone, vertical, handheld at chest height. Start outside, physically walk in and keep walking until you are at the table. Never stop, never turn to a person, never let anyone acknowledge you. Once you arrive, cover the conversation like a bystander: over-the-shoulder one way, reverse the other way, a third angle down the table. Ordinary lens, no zooms, no ramps.
- **Light:** whatever the room has. Big windows, daylight, no fill, no lamps brought in. The outside beat wants open daylight so the cut inside feels like entering.
- **Wardrobe:** exactly what the group would actually wear for this — polos, button-downs, nothing coordinated and nothing styled. Mismatched is the point; matching outfits kill the "real" read.
- **Cast:** a real group having a real conversation. No script, no lines to camera, no performance — nobody in this needs to be able to act. Let them talk and film it.
- **Props:** whatever the routine actually produces on the table — printed pages, notebooks, a document someone holds up while speaking. Do not add props that the conversation would not have.
- **Text:** the opening question pinned top-centre for the first half, then swapped once to the naming line. Dialogue subtitles start at the moment the first real sentence lands, small and low-centre, verbatim, one clause at a time.
