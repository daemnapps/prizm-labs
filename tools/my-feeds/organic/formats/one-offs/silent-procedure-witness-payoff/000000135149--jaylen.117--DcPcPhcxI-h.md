**Why it works**
The whole first act builds a visible change while the subject sits there looking sceptical, so the viewer has already agreed something happened before anyone comments on it. Then the cut hands the verdict to a person who was not part of the shoot — her noticing is the only claim the video ever makes, and a reaction from someone who didn't sign up for it is the one endorsement that can't be written.

**What carries the value**
The second person's unprompted reaction to the finished result. Nothing on screen ever claims anything — the change is held in frame until someone who wasn't part of the shoot notices it and comes over, and that noticing is the entire argument. The product label is legible in two beats of act one and then never mentioned again.

**What the frames do not show** — do not fill these in from imagination:
- Whether anyone speaks, and what is said. There is no audio evidence here and no subtitles on screen. The caption implies dialogue in act two; the frames only show her mouth open at 15.69s.
- Whether there are cuts between the six sampled beats, and whether act two is one continuous take or several. Act two's framing is consistent across all three beats but that is not proof of a single take.
- Whether the practitioner is a real dentist, and whether the clinic is real or a set. It reads clinical; that's all the frames establish.
- Any before-state. There is no shot of the untreated area before the product goes on — the video opens with the jar already being offered.
- Who the woman is. The caption says marriage and the frames show a ring; nothing else about her is established.

**Shoot it**
Two people, two locations, a phone. No performance required from anyone.

- *Act one camera* — hold the phone close and at the reclined subject's own eye level, not above them. Stay handheld and let it drift with the hands; do not put it on a tripod and do not cut away. Three beats: the offer, the product on, the working step. Get the label readable in at least one of them by having the operator hold the jar up beside the subject's face — it is never named out loud, so the label is the only product identification in the piece.
- *Act two camera* — hard cut to arm's length front camera, subject's face filling the frame, treated area full in shot. Hold it. Do not reframe when the second person arrives; let them walk into the background of the shot you're already holding.
- *Light* — act one is flat overhead clinical light, act two is daylight from a window behind. Do not match them. The lighting change is what sells the cut as a real change of day and place.
- *Wardrobe* — act one puts the subject in the room's own uniform (paper bib, gown, apron — whatever the setting supplies) and the operator in scrubs and gloves. Act two puts him in a plain t-shirt, no styling. The wardrobe change is doing the same job as the light: proving act two is a different day.
- *Casting the reactor* — this is the one thing you cannot fake and the one thing the video lives on. Don't direct them and don't tell them where to stand. Start rolling, let them walk through, and use the take where they actually look. If they hit their mark and perform, the format is dead.
- *Order of the three act-two beats* — they only work in this order: result held with the reactor unaware in the background → reactor notices → reactor arrives. Losing the "unaware" beat costs you the whole turn.
