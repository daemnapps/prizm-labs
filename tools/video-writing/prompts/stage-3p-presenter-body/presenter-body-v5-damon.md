You are writing the body that plays after a hook. The body is a proven ad that already sold. Keep its beats in its order and every claim it makes. Throw away its sentences. Hand the story to one presenter and write what comes out of their mouth when they tell it. They're telling a friend in their chair, not reading an ad.

## What you get

- **THE WINNER**: the proven ad's numbered verbatim transcript (T1, T2…). Its beats and claims are the body. Its sentences are not. `{winner_transcript}`
- **THE PRESENTER**: who delivers it, their job, their world, how they talk. `{presenter}`
- **THE HOOK'S LAST FRAME**: where the hook stops, so line one can pick up inside it. `{hook_last_frame}`
- **THE PERSON WATCHING**: the avatar's own words about the problem, with ids. `{avatar_language}`
- **THEIR CULTURE**: the avatar's culture map: where they spend time, what they watch, how they talk. `{culture_map}`
- **THE PRODUCT**: the only source for what the product is and does. `{product_facts}`
- **THE RULINGS**: the brand's standing rulings, word for word. They win over everything here. `{rulings}`
- **THE ENDING**: the call to action the winner uses. `{cta}`

## The two things that do not move

1. **The beats, in the winner's order.** Identification, what they tried, why it failed, the mechanism, the product, the demo, the proof, the guarantee, the scarcity, the ask: every beat the winner has, you have. Each line names the T-numbers it carries.
2. **The claims, exactly, said once.** Every number, name, study, ingredient, spec, guarantee term and bonus keeps its facts. Never add a claim the winner does not make. Never soften one. A claim the winner says twice, you say once. Each proof story keeps its person, their problem, how long it took and the result, in one or two lines.

**Everything else is the presenter's.** That means the sentences, the order of words inside a beat, the jokes, the asides, the comparisons, who they talk to, and how long they stay on a thing.

## Whose story it is

0. **It's the presenter's story, not the founder's.** The presenter never talks about the person who made the product, never says "the guy who made this" and never retells how it was invented. Turn the winner's beats into the presenter's own experience:
   - The **pain** belongs to the people in their world: the regulars, the guy in the chair, what they see every day. It can also be the presenter's own pain.
   - **What was tried and why it failed** is what their people walk in having tried.
   - The **mechanism** is what the presenter knows from doing the job, and the study can stay as something they read or were shown.
   - The **product** is what they use and hand people.
   - The **proof** is the customers whose before and after they show, by name and footage. It's never the founder's own result.
   - **Drop every origin-story claim:** who built it, who helped, the family, the pre-order, the founder's years and the founder's healing. That is the one place a winner's claim may be dropped. Product facts, the mechanism, the proof stories and the offer all stay.

## How the presenter tells it

3. **The copy test.** No line may carry more than six words in a row from the winner. The only exceptions are a claim's own fixed words: a name, a number, a guarantee term or an ingredient. If a line still sounds like the winner with a few words swapped, rewrite it from the beat, not from the sentence.
4. **Their mouth, their logic.** Write what this person would actually say, in the order they'd say it. A gossip starts with "okay so" and buries the point in the middle. A preacher builds to it. A barber says it while the clippers run. Use their markers, their language mixing and their filler where they'd really use them. Don't write a language-coded word into every line. Real people use them in bursts.
5. **The aside is the spice.** Each section gets at least one thing only this presenter would say:
   - a tangent about their own life or their regulars;
   - an over-share;
   - a client they can't name ("I can't say who, but he plays on Sundays");
   - a line to the person in their chair, and that person reacting;
   - a callback to the hook.

   The aside always lands back on the beat, and it never carries a claim of its own.
6. **Absurd and hyper-specific beats general.** Name the exact object, the exact moment, the exact place. That means the brand of the drugstore bag, the hour of the night, the seat at the game, the thing on the shop TV. A comparison has to be one this person would reach for, from their world or the avatar's culture map, never a stock phrase. Pull from the avatar's own words where they fit, and quote the id.
7. **They react to their own story.** They get disgusted at the bad part, smug at the science, hyped at the proof, and protective of the viewer at the close. The feeling changes between sections. It's never one flat level of excited.
8. **Describe the experience, not the problem.** Show what the problem does to a person's life in moments the viewer has lived: the hiding, the timing, the ritual, the embarrassment, the place it happens. One moment like this per section, never a list.
9. **Spoken, not written.** Each thought gets one breath. Use a full stop only where the thought ends, `...` for a breath in the middle of a thought, and a comma for a breath that keeps going. No dashes, semicolons, parentheses or brackets in the Said column. Contractions on. Never chop one thought into a row of short statements. Then do the friend test on every line: would this person say this, in these words, to someone in their chair?
10. **Length: the whole ad runs 3 to 5 minutes, hook included (V25).** The hook takes about 20 seconds, so the body gets **550 to 800 spoken words, aim for 700**, read at a natural fast pace of about 180 to 200 words a minute. Over 800 words is a fail. Write to the budget from the first line:
    - Every beat keeps one strongest line, and the winner's padding goes.
    - An aside replaces a winner line in the same beat. It never sits on top of one.
    - The ingredient list, the spec list and the bonus stack are each said once, quickly.
    - Never cut a beat to save words. Cut words inside every beat.

## The scenes (V35)

11. **A scene is a set-up, never a line.** One place, one camera set-up, a clear START and a clear STOP, with the beats and every line said in it played inside. Plan the ad as about 8 to 12 scenes for 3 to 5 minutes, as the story needs. Several lines in one set-up are ONE scene. A new scene starts only when the place, the set-up or the moment really changes.
12. **Inside a scene, the camera and the action carry it.** Write the beats between the start and the stop: angle changes, a push-in, what the hands do, who reacts. Picture changes inside a set-up are beats, never new scenes (V10).
13. **An insert is its own row only when it is real or it must be seen.** Real means footage we own (the product in use, a before-and-after) or the presenter's swipe's own shot. "Must be seen" means a beat the viewer cannot get without its own picture, the absurd hyper-specific object (V24). Anything else is a beat inside the scene.
14. **Scene one is the join.** It starts inside the hook's last frame, with the same place, the same people and the same mood, and pulls into the presenter's world. It never explains the hook.
15. **The demo is one unbroken scene in the same light.** Say so.
16. **The ending is the winner's ending, in the presenter's voice.** It's the button to the offer page, and the lane's ask is the last thing said. No price unless the rulings allow it.
17. **Every scene and insert names its swipe shot when the swipe has one.** THE PRESENTER'S SWIPE is the frame map of the video this presenter's world comes from. It has one row per shot: `K##`, its start and end in seconds, who is in it and what they do.
    - Pick the shot whose set-up or action matches. Write it as `K## m:ss.ss-m:ss.ss`, using that shot's own start and end, never times that run past its end.
    - Write `none` when no shot fits: someone talking straight to camera, a place the swipe never shows, or footage we own.
    - Never pick a shot under 2 seconds for action that carries the scene.

## What you return

Return a table with one row per SCENE (and per insert that earns its own row), in order:

| # | T-numbers | Swipe shot | What we see | Said | How it's said |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **What we see:** `START:` the one picture the scene opens on (place, framing, who, the prop in hand) · `BEATS:` what happens between, in order (the angle changes, the action, who reacts) · `STOP:` where it lands. Write STOP only as "same set-up" when the scene ends where it started.
- **Said:** every line said in the scene, in order, as she says them, one breath per thought.
- **How it's said:** the scene's performance: the feeling, where it turns, where the weight falls, where the eyes go.

Then add these lines:
- **Built on:** the winner, the presenter, and the join.
- **Scenes:** the count of scenes and of inserts, and one line on why each insert earns its own row.
- **Length:** the body's spoken word count (the Said column only) and the minutes at 190 words a minute. It must land between 550 and 800 words.
- **Copy test:** the count of lines that keep more than six winner words in a row outside a claim's fixed words. It should be 0. Name any line that fails, and say why.
