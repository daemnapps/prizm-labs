Where the name comes from

Light, taken apart.

In 1666 Isaac Newton closed the shutters, let one beam of sunlight into a dark room, and put a prism in its way. Everyone believed the glass was colouring the light. He proved the colours had been in the light all along.

White is every colour at once

The prism doesn't paint anything. It bends each colour a little differently, so the ones travelling together finally come apart.

One colour stays one colour

Newton let a single colour through a second prism. It bent, but it didn't split again. Each colour was its own thing the whole time.

And back to white

Gather the spectrum through a lens and it comes back as white light. Taken apart and put back together, nothing is lost.

How to use them

Rules, so the set stays a set.

  • One object per surface. Two in one frame reads as a product shot, not a brand.
  • Never on white. They are lit for bone; on white the contact shadow disappears and they float wrongly.
  • Never recoloured. Chrome takes its colour from what is around it. Put the spectrum near it instead — a prism hairline, a hued card edge.
  • Scale is free. Impossibly large beside type is the surreal half of the system doing its job. Impossibly small is just a bullet point.
  • The shadow stays. It is the only shadow on a page, and it is what makes the object real rather than pasted.
  • A new object earns its place by standing for something no existing one does — then it gets shot the same way: one object, three-quarter view, hard key upper right, bone ground.
Full-resolution originals are kept out of this repo — it is a toolkit, not an asset library. The 900px WebPs here are the web set.

The spark: Cosmos: A Spacetime Odyssey, “Hiding in the Light” (2014), which tells how people learned to take light apart. The experiment: Isaac Newton's prism experiments, begun in 1666 and published in Opticks (1704). The dark room above is a real simulation: Snell's law at every face, with glass that bends violet more than red.
