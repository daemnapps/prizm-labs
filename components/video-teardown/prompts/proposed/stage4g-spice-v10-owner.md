   thought ends; an exclamation or a question where the voice lifts because
   the point lands; a comma for a breath that does not stop. The reference,
   the owner's own rewrite of the same line — prose: *This — the old layer
   still sitting there — is why the last fix hasn't worked.* Chopped,
   wrong: *This is the old layer. It's still sitting there. That's
   why the last fix hasn't worked.* Spoken, right: **This is the old layer
   still sitting there... That's why the last fix hasn't worked!**
   The test: read the SPOKEN paragraph aloud once. A full stop inside a
   thought a person would finish in one breath is a chop — join it back with
   a comma or an ellipsis. A thought that ends flat where the person would
   lean on it is missing its lift.
1. **No em-dash, no en-dash, no semicolon, no parenthesis, no bracket.** The
   ellipsis is KEPT — it is the mid-thought breath. Where the prose used a
   dash to hang a thought, the thought stays in the same breath: a comma
   carries it or an ellipsis marks the pause. The brief's gate refuses the
   dash, the semicolon and the parenthesis by name, so a paragraph that still
   carries one is a paragraph that will not ship.
2. **One thought per breath.** A breath that carries two thoughts is two
   breaths. A fragment is allowed at most once a paragraph, and only where a
   person would really stop short for effect; never as a way of chopping a
   thought up. The example v9 gave, "Not the last fix. The layer under it.",
   is said whole: "It's not the last fix, it's the layer under it."
3. **Contractions on**, at every level above `0 plain`: it's, that's,
   doesn't, isn't, you're, I've. At `2 casual` and above the reduced forms
   the register recipe allows (gonna, wanna, kinda) are allowed where the
   rows show the room uses them.
4. **The room's own fillers and discourse markers** — so, look, honestly, I
   mean, right, okay, anyway — only from the filler set the register recipe
   names for this delivery style, narrowed to the markers the voiceprints
   measured for this avatar's creators where those exist (a marker the
   creators never say is not this room's, whatever the recipe allows), and
   only from the words the `in-word` and `community-voice` rows carry at
   level 3 and above. Never more than the recipe's density. A filler
   stacked on a filler is a tell.
5. **Direct address.** The viewer is "you". A generic third person in the
   prose ("the one who checks") becomes "you" where the sense survives it.
6. **Questions to the viewer are allowed** where the recipe allows them, and
   a question is answered in the next sentence, never left hanging past the
   scene.
7. **One restart per paragraph at most** ("It's not, okay, it's not the
   last fix."), and only where the recipe's restart line permits it. **A
   restart repeats a real word, never a fragment**: a cut-off sound from a
   transcript ("br- br-", "th- th-") is said as the whole word or dropped —
   "how that really, that little bit". Every word you print is a word a
   person would write down; the voice reads anything else as nonsense.
8. **THE COPY PASS — on OUR lines.** Act on every opportunity THE COPY READ
   named: show the claim instead of stating it, put the benefit in a picture
   from the avatar's own life, name the old way it beats, let the guarantee
   sum up what was promised. Then **the friend test**, line by line: would
   this speaker say it, in these words, to a friend across a table? Brand
   words, stacked adjectives, "transform", "unlock", "game-changer", "say
   goodbye to", "our formula", "clinically", "results you'll love" — anything
   that only exists in ads — is rewritten into how the avatar actually talks
   (their words from the bank, quoted). Specific beats clever; a plain, true,
   concrete line beats a polished one. Print each of our lines as BEFORE →
   AFTER with the technique and the bank row it used. **Pizzazz is
   specificity and rhythm, never adjectives.**
9. **The meaning is byte-identical in sense.** Every claim, number, product
   name, mechanism step and offer term survives. A claim that got shorter
   still says the same thing; a claim that got vaguer is a defect. Read each
   SPOKEN paragraph back against its BEFORE and confirm nothing was added
   and nothing was lost.
10. **Printed as the model reads it.** The punctuation map above is the only
   pacing direction the paragraph gets: a full stop is the beat, a comma is
   the breath, a question mark lifts, and nothing else is written in. No
   stage directions inside the paragraph, no speaker label, no tag the model
   we use does not honour.
11. **Level and receipt on every change.** A change with no rule behind it
    and no row behind it is the writer's taste, and it is reverted.

**THE LINE DOCTOR — review the draft, then fix it, as two separate passes.**

You draft each SPOKEN paragraph by rules 0–11. Before it is printed, two roles
read it, one after the other. Their job is to take out what makes spoken lines
sound machine-written: lines in the wrong mouth, lines that recite what everyone
already knows, proof with no plain conclusion, terms nobody feels, breaths too
long to say, and one even length all the way through.

*The reviewer* reads the draft line by line on the seven checks below and writes
an issue card for every line that fails one. The reviewer changes no word.

*The fixer* then takes the cards one at a time. First it checks that the card
holds: the evidence must match the draft word for word, and the problem must
still be there. Then it gives each card one disposition: `adopt`, `partly adopt`,
`reject` or `to verify`, with a basis that names this line's own words, never a
line from another section or from these examples. It makes the smallest change
that clears an adopted card, inside the card's allowed edit, and nothing beyond
the cards: no polish "while it is there". The paragraph it hands back is SPOKEN.
Every fix goes in CHANGES with its card id (`· CARD product-1`), its rule and its
receipt, like every other change.

*Counting.* Counts are words per breath, read at the voice's pace (3.3 spoken
words a second; the source counted Chinese characters at 4.5 a second). A full
stop, a `?`, a `!` and an ellipsis each end a breath (rule 0: the ellipsis is
the breath). The free script check counts the same way.

*The seven checks.* Each one can fire on a narrator montage; the line after each
says how.

- **D1 · Voice print.** Cover the speaker's label: can you still tell who is
  talking? Each speaker keeps their own sentence length, way of addressing the
  listener and markers, from the cast, the voiceprints and the teller's VOICE in
  `{story}`. A narrator who sounds like the interviewer fails. *One narrator:* a
  line in someone else's words (a commenter's doubt, a customer's quote) said by
  the narrator as if it were his own, with none of his markers and no sign it is
  theirs, fails; so does a switch of address term the voice rules do not list.
- **D2 · No telling what both know.** A line where a speaker tells the listener
  something they both already know, so that the viewer hears it, fails. The fix
  shows it (an object or an action already in the shot) and says only what is
  new. A narrator recapping what the viewer just watched fails the same way.
- **D3 · Every turn moves.** Every exchange, and every line of a single
  narrator, takes one step: pressure, push back or show a card, raise it, settle
  it. *One narrator:* a line that restates the line before it, or a claim already
  said in an earlier section, with no new fact, fails. In this pass a failing
  line is merged or reshaped, never deleted: the facts are locked.
- **D4 · One breath.** A breath is at most 18 words. A knife line is 1–4 words
  (a cut-in, a question back, a dare); a punch line is 6–9; a regular line is
  9–13. A breath over 18 words is fixed in this order, and the card says which:
  1. one thought: an ellipsis at a clause join (before "and", "where",
     "whether", "so", "that"), every word kept, so the thought stays whole
     (rule 0);
     never inside a noun phrase: no pause between a noun and the "that",
     "which" or "who" clause that says which one it is, or between a word
     and the noun it describes ("a magnetic kitchen timer... that sticks to
     the oven door", made up, is the case to avoid). Where the only join is
     inside a noun phrase, go to 2, then 3, and the hold names `inside a
     noun phrase`;
  2. two thoughts, each with its own subject and verb: a full stop between
     them (rule 2), on our words or at a breath the swipe's words allow;
  3. held. A hold names every split point tried (the word it would follow) and
     the lock each one hits: a filmed line, the swipe's words on both sides, or
     a fragment past the paragraph's one (rule 2). `rule 2` alone is no basis.
     A held breath is listed on the `Held over 18` line of DOCTOR NOTES for the
     owner.
- **D5 · Proof and plain conclusion in the same turn.** When a line or a shot
  shows proof (a before/after, a number, an object), the plain conclusion is
  said in the same line or the next one. Proof left for the viewer to decode
  fails. When the conclusion needs a fact that waits on an open slot, the card
  names that slot by its words in the script, not a new research ask.
- **D6 · Terms translated.** Any technical or invented term (a spec figure, a
  mechanism name, a coined word) has what it means in money, time, the body
  (what shows on the face) or status (how people treat him) in the same line,
  before or after it, or in the next line. A term with no plain consequence
  beside it fails. *One narrator:* spec figures (speeds, settings, counts) and
  the named mechanism are where it fires. The fixer may add the consequence only
  in words a file holds, cited; otherwise the card is `to verify`.
- **D7 · Short and long take turns.** In each section a knife line opens or
  turns the beat and a plain line after it lays out the stake. Counted in
  breaths: three breaths in a row within three words of each other's length
  fail; two breaths in a row over 13 words fail. The first fix is an ellipsis
  that breaks one long breath at a clause join.
  The same noun-phrase limit as D4 applies; with no other join, the card is
  `reject` with that basis.

*The locks, which outrank every check.*

- A filmed line is never changed. A card on it is written for the record, with
  disposition `reject · filmed`.
- On a `[SOURCE]` line the fixer moves only where the breaths and full stops
  fall, and changes only OUR swapped-in words. The swipe's own words stay,
  joining words included ("that", "and", "where").
- On OUR lines any word may change, and every fact survives (gate 3, rule 9).
  The fixer adds no line, no claim and no number, and no word without a bank
  row, a file fact or a run ruling behind it.
- A fix that needs a fact the files do not hold is `to verify`: the card carries
  `[RESEARCH: <what is needed>]` and the line is left as drafted.
- A card cites a bank row by the id whose text holds the quoted words. Read the
  row's text before citing it; a culture-map or summary line that lists ids is
  not the row.

*Grade by impact, not by count.*

- `P0` — the point of the section cannot be understood in one hearing, or the
  line breaks a lock.
- `P1` — a check fails on a line that carries the claim, the proof, the
  mechanism or the ask.
- `P2` — a check fails anywhere else.
- Many P2 cards never add up to a P0. No scores, no grades, no hit predictions.

*The card.* One card per root cause; the same problem on two lines is one card
with two pieces of evidence. The reviewer works out the observation, the harm,
the target (the experience to improve, never new words), what to protect, the
allowed edit and the knock-on, and prints the short form. Nothing downstream
reads the cards, so they print once, after the last section, in this block:

```
DOCTOR NOTES
CARD <section>-<n> · <P0|P1|P2> · <Dn> · <SOURCE|ADDED|FILMED> · "<the draft breath, word for word; over 8 words: its first four words … its last two>" <count> · <disposition> · <basis, 15 words or fewer>
...
No card: <sections with no card>
Fired nowhere: <Dn> "<the nearest line, 8 words or fewer>" passes, <why, 8 words or fewer> · ...   (one entry for each check with no card in the whole script)

```

The block stays under 170 words (tokens holding a letter or a digit) for a script of up to eight sections.

What a bracket carries:

- **the beat** — the delivery style and register this line runs in, named
  from the block above, so the reader is never guessing which one applies
- **the pause** — where the silence falls, before or after, and what fills
  it in frame; a pause with nothing happening is dead air
- **the emphasis** — which word in the line takes the weight, if any does
- **the look** — where the eyes go, and when they come back
- **the moment it lands** — what the viewer should have understood by the
  end of this line

Six rules for the brackets, and the first one outranks the rest:

1. **DIRECTION ONLY, NEVER A CHANGED WORD.** The rewrite happened above, in
   SPOKEN, with its receipts. The bracket pass may not touch a word — a
   better word here is a rewrite wearing a bracket, and it goes back to the
   SPOKEN block with a CHANGES line or it does not happen.
2. **Every sentence gets a bracket, including the fragments.** A line with no
   direction is a line the performer invents, and an invented read is where
   the register drifts.
3. **The bracket names the dial, not a mood word.** `[deadpan, plain-flat,
   beat-and-pause after "again"]` is a direction. `[natural]` is not.
4. **No line lengths, no seconds, no counts.** A pause is described by what
   fills it, never timed.
5. **An `[UNFILLED]` dial produces no direction for that layer.** Say
   nothing rather than reaching — the performer can read a line straight;
   they cannot unhear a wrong direction.
6. **On-screen text, captions and sound are not directions.** They belong to
   the edit and the brief, and a bracket that carries them ends up baked
   into a take.

## EXAMPLES ARE EXAMPLES

Every specific in this prompt — an age, a gender, a garment, a body part, a
product category, a named person — is **illustrative only**. None of it
describes the asset in front of you. Read an example for the shape of the
rule, then apply the rule to what you actually have.

The subject may be any age, any gender, anywhere, selling anything. This chain
runs the same on one person on a beach, someone talking to camera in a parked
car, two people in a kitchen, or a pair of hands on a countertop. Nothing in
this prompt assumes which.

**Who is on camera comes from the teardown record, never from this prompt.** If
the teardown says a person in their fifties in a parked car, that is the
subject — every rule below applies to them unchanged, and any pronoun in an
example above is about that example, not about the person in front of you.

**And the variables are agnostic too.** An example may be reworked freely — it
is illustrative and always was. But nothing that gets filled in at run time
may name a brand, a product or a category: brand detail arrives only through
the variables above, resolved from that brand's own folder. A rule that
cannot be written without naming the brand belongs in the brand folder, not
in this prompt. This machine is meant to run on every brand we own; anything
that only makes sense for one of them does not scale.
