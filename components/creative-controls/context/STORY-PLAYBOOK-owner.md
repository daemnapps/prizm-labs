# Story Playbook

The flow every ad runs, and the story layer of the creative controls
(`components/creative-controls/controls.json`); awareness and sections are the marketing rulebook's
(`components/marketing-doctrine/frameworks.json`). Page: see `components/image-teardown/context/artifacts.md`.

## The flow

1. **Avatar + product**: Who it is for and what the product honestly does (brand files · research round · culture map)
2. **The idea**: Door A: a swipe that already works. Door B: our own story, no swipe (swipe pools + teardown · research bank (concept)) — the owner picks
3. **Awareness**: Where the ad is allowed to begin (marketing rulebook) — the owner picks
4. **Arc**: The story's shape; each beat is a section (Story Playbook) — the owner picks
5. **Format + style**: What kind of ad and what it looks like (format and style banks · format playbook)
6. **Writing**: Hooks, script, audit, spice, the brief; dials, spice and culture read in (video writing (door B: from scratch))
7. **Brief review**: Is this the ad (the board) — the owner OKs
8. **Audio first**: The song or the voice, made from the brief (video production) — the owner OKs
9. **Cast, rooms, scene pictures**: Who is in it, the 3D sets, one picture per scene (casting · the board) — the owner OKs each
10. **Hand-off**: Clips, B-roll, effects, the cut (hand-off folder) — a creator
11. **Launch**: Review, names, the approval card (ad review · naming · Meta card) — the owner's click

## Awareness → arcs

- **Unaware**: Does not know the desire or the need exists — or will not admit it. Begins: At the person — a portrait, nothing sold in the opening at all. Arcs: Icarus, Story circle, Kishotenketsu, But / therefore, Cold open, Mystery box, Escalation ladder, Micro-drama, Two sides, Unreliable narrator
- **Problem-Aware**: Feels a need but has not connected it to any product category. Begins: At the problem — the need, stated and felt, before anything is offered. Arcs: Person in a hole, Icarus, Cinderella, Story circle, Save the cat beats, Hero's journey, But / therefore, Epiphany bridge, Micro-drama, Unreliable narrator
- **Solution-Aware**: Carries the desire and knows a result is possible, but does not know a product delivers it. Begins: At the desire or the result — the product arrives after the proof. Arcs: Person in a hole, Cinderella, Oedipus, Save the cat beats, Hero's journey, But / therefore, Epiphany bridge
- **Product-Aware**: Knows the product exists but is not sold on it. Begins: At the product, but on the gap between knowing it and wanting it. Arcs: Rags to riches, But / therefore, Transformation reveal
- **Most Aware**: Knows the product, wants it, simply has not bought yet. Begins: At the product itself — name and terms in the opening beat. Arcs: Rags to riches, Transformation reveal

## Arcs

The awareness level picks the arc (best_for). Every beat of the arc holds one section from the marketing doctrine's list, so the arc IS the section plan. After the last beat, the close follows as the awareness level asks (offer, call-to-action, urgency where it is true). One arc per piece; a borrowed swipe keeps its arc unless the brief says why it changes.

### Person in a hole (`person-in-a-hole`)

Fall then rise. The most-shared emotional shape in a 1,700-story corpus; the fall makes the rise feel earned.

Beats → sections: Fine, normal life (Identification) → Falls in: the problem hits, it gets worse (Problem) → Rock bottom (Agitation) → The way out (the product as the rope) (Unique mechanism) → Higher than where it started (Transformation)

Source: Vonnegut, 'Shapes of Stories' lecture ('man in a hole'); Reagan et al. 2016, EPJ Data Science

### Rags to riches (`rags-to-riches`)

Rise. Pure aspiration with no dip; it carries on momentum and a strong before-and-after picture.

Beats → sections: Low start, shown in a specific detail (Identification) → Each step up, each one visible (Proof) → The new life (Transformation)

Source: Reagan et al. 2016 (the six core arcs)

### Icarus (`icarus`)

Rise then fall. Schadenfreude plus the fear of being that person: it sells prevention and is the arc of most public scandals.

Beats → sections: The lead is winning (Identification) → The lead ignores the warning sign (Problem) → It catches up, publicly (Agitation)

Source: Reagan et al. 2016; Greek myth

### Cinderella (`cinderella`)

Rise, fall, rise higher. The arc with the most reliably positive response in the corpus; the middle loss sets up the end.

Beats → sections: Things start going right (Identification) → Taken away: the setback (Agitation) → Back, and better than before (Transformation)

Source: Vonnegut; Reagan et al. 2016

### Oedipus (`oedipus`)

Fall, rise, fall. Shows the failed solutions, so the real one lands as the only one left.

Beats → sections: The problem (Problem) → The false fix that seems to work (Failed solutions) → It comes back worse (Agitation)

Source: Reagan et al. 2016; Schwartz on failed solutions

### Story circle (`story-circle`)

A circle out and back, changed. Every satisfying story leaves and comes back changed; the circle makes a 30 s ad feel complete.

Beats → sections: You: in a comfort zone (Identification) → Need: wants something (Problem) → Go: enters an unfamiliar situation (Failed solutions) → Search: adapts to it (Root cause) → Find: gets what was wanted (Unique mechanism) → Take: pays a heavy price (Objections) → Return: goes back to where it started (Proof) → Change: having changed (Transformation)

Source: Dan Harmon, the story circle (after Campbell)

### Save the cat beats (`save-the-cat`)

Three acts with a midpoint and a dark night. The commercial-film standard; its last frame mirrors its first so the change can be seen.

Beats → sections: Opening image (Hook) → Theme stated (Identification) → Catalyst (Problem) → Debate (Objections) → Break into two (Solution) → Fun and games (Proof) → Midpoint (false win or false loss) (Failed solutions) → Bad guys close in (Agitation) → All is lost (Agitation) → Dark night (Root cause) → Break into three (Unique mechanism) → Finale (Product) → Final image (the opening, reversed) (Transformation)

Source: Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (2005)

### Hero's journey (`heros-journey`)

Departure, ordeal, return with the gift. The viewer is the hero; the brand is the mentor, never the hero.

Beats → sections: Ordinary world (Identification) → Call (the problem) (Problem) → Refusal (Objections) → Meeting the mentor (the side character with the fix) (Unique mechanism) → The ordeal (Agitation) → The reward (Transformation) → Return with the elixir (Call to action)

Source: Joseph Campbell (1949); Christopher Vogler, The Writer's Journey; Donald Miller, StoryBrand

### Kishotenketsu (`kishotenketsu`)

Set-up, development, twist, reconciliation. Holds attention with surprise instead of conflict; built for the absurd and the surreal.

Beats → sections: Ki: introduce (Hook) → Sho: develop (Identification) → Ten: the unrelated twist (Unique mechanism) → Ketsu: the twist and the set-up click together (Product)

Source: Classical Chinese and Japanese four-part composition

### But / therefore (`but-therefore`)

Every beat caused by the last. Beats joined by 'and then' feel like a list; joined by 'but' and 'therefore' they feel like a story, and nobody scrolls in the middle of one.

Beats → sections: This happens (Hook) → BUT this goes wrong (Problem) → THEREFORE the lead does this (Solution) → BUT (Objections) → THEREFORE (Proof) → until the end (Transformation)

Source: Trey Parker & Matt Stone, NYU masterclass (2011)

### Cold open (`cold-open`)

The climax first, then how we got here. The open question ('how did it get here?') holds the viewer through the set-up they would otherwise skip.

Beats → sections: The most dramatic moment, unexplained (Hook) → Rewind: 'three weeks earlier' (Identification) → The story to that moment (Problem) → Past it: the resolution (Transformation)

Source: Horace, Ars Poetica (in medias res); TV cold-open practice

### Mystery box (`mystery-box`)

A question first, the answer last. An unanswered question is felt as a gap that has to be closed; the viewer watches to close it.

Beats → sections: An image or line that cannot be explained yet (Hook) → Clues, each one raising the question (Root cause) → The reveal, which recasts everything (Unique mechanism)

Source: J. J. Abrams, 'The mystery box' (TED 2007); Loewenstein 1994

### Epiphany bridge (`epiphany-bridge`)

The teller's story leads to the same realisation the viewer needs. A viewer accepts a belief discovered inside a story far more easily than one they are told.

Beats → sections: Backstory: where I was (Identification) → Desire: what I wanted (Problem) → The wall: what stopped me (Failed solutions) → The epiphany: what I found out (Unique mechanism) → The plan (Solution) → The conflict while trying it (Objections) → The result, and who I am now (Transformation)

Source: Russell Brunson, Expert Secrets (2017)

### Escalation ladder (`escalation-ladder`)

One absurd idea, pushed further every beat. Each step is surprising but follows the rule the viewer learned on step one, so it feels inevitable and funny.

Beats → sections: Find the one unusual thing (Hook) → Do it again, bigger (Agitation) → Bigger (Agitation) → Biggest, past the point of sense (Agitation) → Snap back to the product (Product)

Source: Upright Citizens Brigade, 'the game' (Besser, Roberts & Walsh, UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual)

### Micro-drama (`micro-drama`)

Hook, conflict, reversal, cliffhanger, every minute. The vertical-drama apps hold viewers for hours with a reversal every 60-90 s; the satisfying comeuppance is the reward.

Beats → sections: Hook: a betrayal or a shock in the first second (Hook) → Conflict out loud (Problem) → A reversal: the power flips (Solution) → A cliffhanger or a satisfying humiliation of the villain (Transformation)

Source: Vertical short-drama practice (2023-2026)

### Two sides (`two-sides`)

The same event told twice, differently. The viewer becomes the judge; judging is engagement, and the gap between versions is the joke.

Beats → sections: One version (Identification) → The other version (or the camera's) (Problem) → The truth, which neither said (Proof)

Source: Kurosawa, Rashomon (1950); reality-TV confessional practice

### Unreliable narrator (`unreliable-narrator`)

The lead tells it one way, the picture shows another. The viewer is ahead of the character, which feels clever and holds the viewer to the moment the character catches up.

Beats → sections: The lead brags or denies (Identification) → The picture quietly contradicts it (Problem) → Everyone but the lead can see it (Agitation) → The lead finally sees it (Solution)

Source: Wayne Booth, The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961)

### Transformation reveal (`transformation-reveal`)

Build-up, then the drop. The held before is the tension; the drop on the beat is the release, and it is the frame people screenshot.

Beats → sections: The before, held long enough to feel (Problem) → The process, sped up or hidden (Unique mechanism) → The drop: the after, on a beat (Transformation)

Source: Short-form transition practice; tension-release (music theory)

## Story devices

Story devices are switches inside the arc: name each one used. A device that is set up must be paid off.

- **Open loop** (Curiosity): Start a story or question and leave it unfinished; an interrupted task stays in the mind until it is closed. How: Open two loops in the first 5 s and close the second one last. _(Zeigarnik 1927)_
- **Curiosity gap** (Curiosity): Show the viewer there is something they do not know yet, and that it is about them. How: Name the gap precisely ('the one thing every pro in the trade knows that you don't') so it feels small enough to close by watching. _(Loewenstein 1994, Psychological Bulletin)_
- **Question hook** (Curiosity): Open on a question the viewer answers in their head. How: Ask the question the viewer is embarrassed to ask out loud. _(Copywriting practice)_
- **Pattern interrupt** (Curiosity): Break what the feed has trained the eye to expect: wrong scale, wrong place, wrong sound. How: Combine it with the curiosity gap so the interrupt asks a question. _(Neuro-linguistic practice; attention research on novelty)_
- **Ticking clock** (Tension): A visible time limit: the date, the meeting, the mirror before leaving the house. How: Put the clock on screen; cut faster as it runs down. _(Screenwriting practice)_
- **Raise the stakes** (Tension): Every beat costs the lead more: from comfort, to a date, to a job, to their name in public. How: Make the last stake social and public: who sees it. _(McKee, Story (1997))_
- **Reversal** (Tension): The situation turns into its opposite: the winner is losing, the bully is humiliated. How: Put the reversal on a cut and a sound hit. _(Aristotle, Poetics)_
- **Recognition** (Tension): The character realises the truth, usually about themselves. How: Show it on the face, held; it is the frame the viewer identifies with. _(Aristotle, Poetics)_
- **Set-up and payoff** (Tension): Something shown early that matters at the end. How: Plant the product or the clue in the first 5 s so the ending feels earned, not sold. _(Chekhov (letters, 1889))_
- **False victory** (Tension): The lead thinks it is fixed; it is not. How: Make the failed fix the competitor's category (the usual tool everyone already tried) and let it fail on screen. _(Snyder 2005)_
- **All is lost** (Tension): The lowest point just before the turn. How: Strip the sound or the colour at the low point; the product arrives after it, never before. _(Snyder 2005)_
- **Twist** (Tension): A late fact that changes the meaning of everything before it. How: The twist must be fair: one clue earlier that the viewer only notices on a second watch (rewatches count). _(Narrative practice)_
- **Cliffhanger** (Tension): End a section on an unresolved peak. How: In a series, end each part on one; in one ad, end each verse on one. _(Serial fiction practice)_
- **Want versus need** (Character): The lead wants one thing (respect, the date); the lead needs another (to stop hiding). How: The ad delivers the want by solving the need. _(McKee 1997; Snyder 2005)_
- **The flaw** (Character): One specific, human flaw the viewer shares: vanity, denial, pride. How: Make the flaw funny, never contemptible; the viewer must like the lead. _(Character-writing practice)_
- **Save the cat moment** (Character): A small kind or relatable act early, so the viewer is on their side. How: Even the villain-of-the-story gets one if it is the lead. _(Snyder 2005)_
- **The villain** (Character): The problem as a character: the tool that fails, the bad advice, the doubter. How: Never a real person or a real competitor by name; a category or a personified thing. _(StoryBrand (Miller 2017); us-vs-them framing)_
- **The mentor** (Character): The side character who hands over the fix: the pro, the elder, the friend. How: The mentor is who the brand plays; the viewer stays the hero. _(Campbell 1949; Miller 2017)_
- **The witness** (Character): Someone else reacts to the problem or the change, so the viewer sees it through their eyes. How: The witness shows what the hero cannot: the friend's wince, the date's double take. _(Film grammar (the reaction shot))_
- **The confession** (Character): The lead admits something to the camera that they hide from everyone else. How: Start on the confession; it is a pattern interrupt and an open loop at once. _(Reality-TV confessional; testimonial practice)_
- **Emotional whiplash** (Emotion): Swing from one emotion to its opposite in one cut: laughing to wincing, shame to pride. How: One swing per 10 s keeps arousal up; arousal drives sharing. _(Berger & Milkman 2012)_
- **Peak and end** (Emotion): People remember the most intense moment and the last moment, not the average. How: Design one peak and make the last 2 s the best feeling in the ad. _(Kahneman, Fredrickson et al. 1993)_
- **The specific detail** (Emotion): One detail so exact it can only be true: the receipt, the timestamp, the brand of the cheap thing that failed. How: One per beat; specific beats general every time. _(Heath & Heath, Made to Stick (2007))_
- **Identity mirror** (Emotion): The viewer sees themselves in the character's exact situation. How: Use the avatar's own words from research, word for word. _(Self-referencing research (Burnkrant & Unnava 1995))_
- **Callback ending** (Payoff): The last frame or line returns to the first, changed. How: Same framing, same line, opposite meaning. _(Snyder 2005 (opening and final image))_
- **The tag** (Payoff): One more joke after the story is over. How: Put the product or the offer in the tag so the laugh carries it. _(Comedy practice (sketch buttons))_
- **The receipt** (Payoff): Hard proof on screen at the moment of doubt: the close-up, the real result, the real date. How: Show it when the viewer's doubt peaks, not at the end. _(Schwartz on verification; DR proof practice)_

## Persuasion triggers

Persuasion triggers are why a story turns into a click. Each row says how far to push and where the line is; the line is a guard, never a dial. A trigger used without its truth (a fake deadline, a fake crowd) is refused.

- **Narrative transportation**: A viewer absorbed in a story argues back less and takes on its beliefs. How: Deliver the claim inside the story, never as a break from it. The line: The claim inside the story must still be true. _(Green & Brock 2000)_
- **Social proof**: People do what people like them are doing. How: Show a crowd of people like the viewer, in the viewer's world, doing it. The line: Real numbers and real customers only. _(Cialdini, Influence (1984))_
- **Authority**: We follow people who look like they know. How: The pro, the doctor, the elder who has seen it all: a role the viewer already trusts. The line: Never a fake credential; an actor is a character, never claimed as a real expert. _(Cialdini 1984)_
- **Scarcity**: Things feel more valuable when they might run out. How: Tie it to a real moment: the season, the event, the real stock. The line: Never a fake countdown or fake stock. _(Cialdini 1984; Worchel et al. 1975)_
- **Loss aversion**: A loss hurts about twice as much as the same gain feels good. How: Frame what the viewer keeps losing: the photos deleted, the plans skipped. The line: No fear about a health problem the viewer does not have. _(Kahneman & Tversky 1979)_
- **Commitment and consistency**: After a small yes, a bigger yes is easier. How: Ask a question the viewer answers yes to in their head, then another, then the click. The line: Never trick anyone into a commitment. _(Cialdini 1984; Freedman & Fraser 1966)_
- **Unity**: We say yes to people who are 'us'. How: Speak as one of their own: their slang, their world, their references. The line: Never fake membership in a real community or culture. _(Cialdini, Pre-Suasion (2016))_
- **The reason why**: A request with a reason gets more yeses, even a thin reason. How: Always give the mechanism: why it works, in one line. The line: The reason must be true. _(Langer, Blank & Chanowitz 1978)_
- **Contrast and anchoring**: A thing is judged against what is shown next to it. How: Show the expensive, painful alternative first, then ours. The line: The anchor must be a real alternative at a real price. _(Tversky & Kahneman 1974)_
- **Future pacing**: Let the viewer picture themselves after, in a specific moment. How: One exact future scene: the photo posted, the compliment received. The line: Never promise a result the product does not give. _(Persuasion practice; mental simulation research (Escalas 2004))_
- **Identity shift**: People act to match who they think they are. How: Sell who the viewer becomes, not the tool: 'the one who never hides anymore'. The line: Never shame who the viewer is now. _(Identity-based motivation (Oyserman 2009))_
- **Reciprocity**: We return favours, even small ones. How: Give something real first: the tip, the free quiz result. The line: A gift with no strings, really. _(Cialdini 1984)_
- **The common enemy**: A shared enemy bonds the viewer to whoever names it. How: The enemy is the problem or the old way, never a person. The line: Never a real competitor by name or a group of people. _(Persuasion practice; us-vs-them framing)_

## The edge

Push:

- Exaggeration and absurdity everyone can see is a joke
- Taboo-adjacent humour: near-curses, innuendo, 'pause'
- Real public stories, told as what was publicly reported, allegations labelled
- Real fears and real embarrassment, shown honestly
- Lookalike archetypes and cultural nods, never the real person
- Uncomfortable truths about the problem, the category and the old way

Never:

- A claim the product cannot back
- A fake testimonial, review, crowd, expert or result presented as real
- A fake deadline, fake stock or fake price
- A real person named, impersonated or defamed
- Mocking a health condition, disability, race, religion or any protected trait
- Minors in anything risky
- A real tragedy used for laughs
- Shame aimed at the viewer instead of at the problem
