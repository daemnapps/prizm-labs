Story Playbook

One flow, the same every time. Each step has its definitions in one place, and every chain reads them from there. Pick the awareness level, the arc follows, the arc gives the sections, and the copy, dials and spice fill them in.

The quality bar (18)
  • the first sung word lands inside 1 s and it is the story's punch line
  • no gap over 2 s between sung words; dead air under 5% (song.py flow)
  • every picture in the swipe's style row — no photoreal person in an animated ad
  • every shot moves: no still, no frozen frame, no held last frame
  • no readable garbage text anywhere on screen (blurred, never punched past)
  • no stranger, no merged faces, no real third-party brand in frame
  • every clip sits under the lyric it illustrates
  • master ≤ -1 dBTP at -14 LUFS
  • the brand line is heard plainly once in the bridge and in the outro
  • the product matches the real photo exactly (shape, colour, label) AND is in the film's style — no real footage in the body; the end card is the real packshot
  • it sounds like a song: a melody and a sung hook, not a rushed list of lines
  • the story is real: real cheating, real scandal, real stakes; never a softened version (the owner, 2026-10-28: "I want the cheating to be real")
  • innuendo and sus pushed right up to the edge line (Story Playbook): double meanings, 'pause', near-curses; never over it
  • every scene has one shocking thing a viewer would screenshot
  • it watches like a music video and a movie at once: the singer's performance shots intercut with the story, shot sizes varied (wide, medium, close), every shot staged and lit like film
  • depth in every scene: built from its 3D room with a real lens and light, never a flat backdrop
  • the clip model is used to its limit: camera moves, physical acting, emotion travelling through the shot; never a held pose
  • upscaled to 1080x1920 before launch; nothing ships at the clip model's working resolution

Awareness picks the arc

The awareness level picks the arc (best_for). Every beat of the arc holds one section from the marketing doctrine's list, so the arc IS the section plan. After the last beat, the close follows as the awareness level asks (offer, call-to-action, urgency where it is true). One arc per piece; a borrowed swipe keeps its arc unless the brief says why it changes.

The arcs

The line is the story's emotional shape, left to right: down is worse, up is better. Each beat names the section it fills.

Person in a hole

Fall then rise.

The most-shared emotional shape in a 1,700-story corpus; the fall makes the rise feel earned.

  1. Fine, normal life Identification
  2. Falls in: the problem hits, it gets worse Problem
  3. Rock bottom Agitation
  4. The way out (the product as the rope) Unique mechanism
  5. Higher than where it started Transformation

Vonnegut, 'Shapes of Stories' lecture ('man in a hole'); Reagan et al. 2016, EPJ Data Science · best for problem aware, solution aware

Rags to riches

Rise.

Pure aspiration with no dip; it carries on momentum and a strong before-and-after picture.

  1. Low start, shown in a specific detail Identification
  2. Each step up, each one visible Proof
  3. The new life Transformation

Reagan et al. 2016 (the six core arcs) · best for product aware, most aware

Icarus

Rise then fall.

Schadenfreude plus the fear of being that person: it sells prevention and is the arc of most public scandals.

  1. The lead is winning Identification
  2. The lead ignores the warning sign Problem
  3. It catches up, publicly Agitation

Reagan et al. 2016; Greek myth · best for unaware, problem aware

Cinderella

Rise, fall, rise higher.

The arc with the most reliably positive response in the corpus; the middle loss sets up the end.

  1. Things start going right Identification
  2. Taken away: the setback Agitation
  3. Back, and better than before Transformation

Vonnegut; Reagan et al. 2016 · best for problem aware, solution aware

Oedipus

Fall, rise, fall.

Shows the failed solutions, so the real one lands as the only one left.

  1. The problem Problem
  2. The false fix that seems to work Failed solutions
  3. It comes back worse Agitation

Reagan et al. 2016; Schwartz on failed solutions · best for solution aware

Story circle

A circle out and back, changed.

Every satisfying story leaves and comes back changed; the circle makes a 30 s ad feel complete.

  1. You: in a comfort zone Identification
  2. Need: wants something Problem
  3. Go: enters an unfamiliar situation Failed solutions
  4. Search: adapts to it Root cause
  5. Find: gets what was wanted Unique mechanism
  6. Take: pays a heavy price Objections
  7. Return: goes back to where it started Proof
  8. Change: having changed Transformation

Dan Harmon, the story circle (after Campbell) · best for unaware, problem aware

Save the cat beats

Three acts with a midpoint and a dark night.

The commercial-film standard; its last frame mirrors its first so the change can be seen.

  1. Opening image Hook
  2. Theme stated Identification
  3. Catalyst Problem
  4. Debate Objections
  5. Break into two Solution
  6. Fun and games Proof
  7. Midpoint (false win or false loss) Failed solutions
  8. Bad guys close in Agitation
  9. All is lost Agitation
  10. Dark night Root cause
  11. Break into three Unique mechanism
  12. Finale Product
  13. Final image (the opening, reversed) Transformation

Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (2005) · best for problem aware, solution aware

Hero's journey

Departure, ordeal, return with the gift.

The viewer is the hero; the brand is the mentor, never the hero.

  1. Ordinary world Identification
  2. Call (the problem) Problem
  3. Refusal Objections
  4. Meeting the mentor (the side character with the fix) Unique mechanism
  5. The ordeal Agitation
  6. The reward Transformation
  7. Return with the elixir Call to action

Joseph Campbell (1949); Christopher Vogler, The Writer's Journey; Donald Miller, StoryBrand · best for problem aware, solution aware

Kishotenketsu

Set-up, development, twist, reconciliation.

Holds attention with surprise instead of conflict; built for the absurd and the surreal.

  1. Ki: introduce Hook
  2. Sho: develop Identification
  3. Ten: the unrelated twist Unique mechanism
  4. Ketsu: the twist and the set-up click together Product

Classical Chinese and Japanese four-part composition · best for unaware

But / therefore

Every beat caused by the last.

Beats joined by 'and then' feel like a list; joined by 'but' and 'therefore' they feel like a story, and nobody scrolls in the middle of one.

  1. This happens Hook
  2. BUT this goes wrong Problem
  3. THEREFORE the lead does this Solution
  4. BUT Objections
  5. THEREFORE Proof
  6. until the end Transformation

Trey Parker & Matt Stone, NYU masterclass (2011) · best for unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware

Cold open

The climax first, then how we got here.

The open question ('how did it get here?') holds the viewer through the set-up they would otherwise skip.

  1. The most dramatic moment, unexplained Hook
  2. Rewind: 'three weeks earlier' Identification
  3. The story to that moment Problem
  4. Past it: the resolution Transformation

Horace, Ars Poetica (in medias res); TV cold-open practice · best for unaware

Mystery box

A question first, the answer last.

An unanswered question is felt as a gap that has to be closed; the viewer watches to close it.

  1. An image or line that cannot be explained yet Hook
  2. Clues, each one raising the question Root cause
  3. The reveal, which recasts everything Unique mechanism

J. J. Abrams, 'The mystery box' (TED 2007); Loewenstein 1994 · best for unaware

Epiphany bridge

The teller's story leads to the same realisation the viewer needs.

A viewer accepts a belief discovered inside a story far more easily than one they are told.

  1. Backstory: where I was Identification
  2. Desire: what I wanted Problem
  3. The wall: what stopped me Failed solutions
  4. The epiphany: what I found out Unique mechanism
  5. The plan Solution
  6. The conflict while trying it Objections
  7. The result, and who I am now Transformation

Russell Brunson, Expert Secrets (2017) · best for problem aware, solution aware

Escalation ladder

One absurd idea, pushed further every beat.

Each step is surprising but follows the rule the viewer learned on step one, so it feels inevitable and funny.

  1. Find the one unusual thing Hook
  2. Do it again, bigger Agitation
  3. Bigger Agitation
  4. Biggest, past the point of sense Agitation
  5. Snap back to the product Product

Upright Citizens Brigade, 'the game' (Besser, Roberts & Walsh, UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual) · best for unaware

Micro-drama

Hook, conflict, reversal, cliffhanger, every minute.

The vertical-drama apps hold viewers for hours with a reversal every 60-90 s; the satisfying comeuppance is the reward.

  1. Hook: a betrayal or a shock in the first second Hook
  2. Conflict out loud Problem
  3. A reversal: the power flips Solution
  4. A cliffhanger or a satisfying humiliation of the villain Transformation

Vertical short-drama practice (2023-2026) · best for unaware, problem aware

Two sides

The same event told twice, differently.

The viewer becomes the judge; judging is engagement, and the gap between versions is the joke.

  1. One version Identification
  2. The other version (or the camera's) Problem
  3. The truth, which neither said Proof

Kurosawa, Rashomon (1950); reality-TV confessional practice · best for unaware

Unreliable narrator

The lead tells it one way, the picture shows another.

The viewer is ahead of the character, which feels clever and holds the viewer to the moment the character catches up.

  1. The lead brags or denies Identification
  2. The picture quietly contradicts it Problem
  3. Everyone but the lead can see it Agitation
  4. The lead finally sees it Solution

Wayne Booth, The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961) · best for unaware, problem aware

Transformation reveal

Build-up, then the drop.

The held before is the tension; the drop on the beat is the release, and it is the frame people screenshot.

  1. The before, held long enough to feel Problem
  2. The process, sped up or hidden Unique mechanism
  3. The drop: the after, on a beat Transformation

Short-form transition practice; tension-release (music theory) · best for product aware, most aware

The sections

The job each beat does. Any arc uses some of them, in its own order.

Hook

The point of entry. One job: force the next beat to be watched. Its content is set by the awareness level and the sophistication stage, not by taste.

Problem

The need named and felt, described in enough specific detail that the audience recognises its own situation before anything is offered.

Failed solutions

The alternatives already tried, each failing at a moment the audience recognises — every named weakness answered immediately by the matching strength.

Root cause

The real reason the problem persists, reframed so it is a different problem than the audience thought, and reached by steps already accepted.

Unique mechanism

How the product produces the result — named, described or featured, at the depth the market's sophistication stage demands. At stage 3 and above the mechanism leads.

Solution

The product in action and the desire fulfilled, stretched across time, carrying the role the audience wants to be seen in.

Product

What the thing actually is — the physical facts, used only to justify price, document quality, promise durability or sharpen the picture, never as the lead sell.

Offer

The terms: price set against a bigger anchor, and the guarantee restating every promise as the final intensification beat.

Call to action

The conclusion of the accepted-step chain — the single step still left once every prior step has been agreed to.

Identification

Who this is for. The call-out that names the viewer to themselves — their situation, their role, their words — before any problem is stated or anything is offered. At unaware this is the only opening allowed; everywhere else it is the beat that earns the right to name the problem.

Agitation

The stakes. What it costs to leave the problem alone, and the future that arrives if nothing changes — the black side of the picture, reached one already-accepted step at a time rather than asserted.

Proof

Evidence placed at the beat where doubt peaks, never front-loaded because it happens to be proof: results, testimonials, authority, demonstration, before and after — and written as selling copy, dramatic and specific, not as a dry fact block.

Transformation

The after. The fulfilled desire pictured — the first-use moment lived, the benefit stretched across weeks and months, and the role the viewer gets to be seen playing once it has happened.

Objections

The flinch answered. The specific reasons not to buy — named in the viewer's own words — redefined or resolved before the ask, never after it.

Urgency

Why now. The reason to act in this moment rather than later — real and receipted: a dated deal, a real limit, a cost that compounds. Invented scarcity is never this section.

Story devices

Story devices are switches inside the arc: name each one used. A device that is set up must be paid off.

Curiosity

Open loop

Start a story or question and leave it unfinished; an interrupted task stays in the mind until it is closed.

HowOpen two loops in the first 5 s and close the second one last.

Zeigarnik 1927

Curiosity gap

Show the viewer there is something they do not know yet, and that it is about them.

HowName the gap precisely ('the one thing every pro in the trade knows that you don't') so it feels small enough to close by watching.

Loewenstein 1994, Psychological Bulletin

Question hook

Open on a question the viewer answers in their head.

HowAsk the question the viewer is embarrassed to ask out loud.

Copywriting practice

Pattern interrupt

Break what the feed has trained the eye to expect: wrong scale, wrong place, wrong sound.

HowCombine it with the curiosity gap so the interrupt asks a question.

Neuro-linguistic practice; attention research on novelty

Tension

Ticking clock

A visible time limit: the date, the meeting, the mirror before leaving the house.

HowPut the clock on screen; cut faster as it runs down.

Screenwriting practice

Raise the stakes

Every beat costs the lead more: from comfort, to a date, to a job, to their name in public.

HowMake the last stake social and public: who sees it.

McKee, Story (1997)

Reversal

The situation turns into its opposite: the winner is losing, the bully is humiliated.

HowPut the reversal on a cut and a sound hit.

Aristotle, Poetics

Recognition

The character realises the truth, usually about themselves.

HowShow it on the face, held; it is the frame the viewer identifies with.

Aristotle, Poetics

Set-up and payoff

Something shown early that matters at the end.

HowPlant the product or the clue in the first 5 s so the ending feels earned, not sold.

Chekhov (letters, 1889)

False victory

The lead thinks it is fixed; it is not.

HowMake the failed fix the competitor's category (the usual tool everyone already tried) and let it fail on screen.

Snyder 2005

All is lost

The lowest point just before the turn.

HowStrip the sound or the colour at the low point; the product arrives after it, never before.

Snyder 2005

Twist

A late fact that changes the meaning of everything before it.

HowThe twist must be fair: one clue earlier that the viewer only notices on a second watch (rewatches count).

Narrative practice

Cliffhanger

End a section on an unresolved peak.

HowIn a series, end each part on one; in one ad, end each verse on one.

Serial fiction practice

Character

Want versus need

The lead wants one thing (respect, the date); the lead needs another (to stop hiding).

HowThe ad delivers the want by solving the need.

McKee 1997; Snyder 2005

The flaw

One specific, human flaw the viewer shares: vanity, denial, pride.

HowMake the flaw funny, never contemptible; the viewer must like the lead.

Character-writing practice

Save the cat moment

A small kind or relatable act early, so the viewer is on their side.

HowEven the villain-of-the-story gets one if it is the lead.

Snyder 2005

The villain

The problem as a character: the tool that fails, the bad advice, the doubter.

HowNever a real person or a real competitor by name; a category or a personified thing.

StoryBrand (Miller 2017); us-vs-them framing

The mentor

The side character who hands over the fix: the pro, the elder, the friend.

HowThe mentor is who the brand plays; the viewer stays the hero.

Campbell 1949; Miller 2017

The witness

Someone else reacts to the problem or the change, so the viewer sees it through their eyes.

HowThe witness shows what the hero cannot: the friend's wince, the date's double take.

Film grammar (the reaction shot)

The confession

The lead admits something to the camera that they hide from everyone else.

HowStart on the confession; it is a pattern interrupt and an open loop at once.

Reality-TV confessional; testimonial practice

Emotion

Emotional whiplash

Swing from one emotion to its opposite in one cut: laughing to wincing, shame to pride.

HowOne swing per 10 s keeps arousal up; arousal drives sharing.

Berger & Milkman 2012

Peak and end

People remember the most intense moment and the last moment, not the average.

HowDesign one peak and make the last 2 s the best feeling in the ad.

Kahneman, Fredrickson et al. 1993

The specific detail

One detail so exact it can only be true: the receipt, the timestamp, the brand of the cheap thing that failed.

HowOne per beat; specific beats general every time.

Heath & Heath, Made to Stick (2007)

Identity mirror

The viewer sees themselves in the character's exact situation.

HowUse the avatar's own words from research, word for word.

Self-referencing research (Burnkrant & Unnava 1995)

Payoff

Callback ending

The last frame or line returns to the first, changed.

HowSame framing, same line, opposite meaning.

Snyder 2005 (opening and final image)

The tag

One more joke after the story is over.

HowPut the product or the offer in the tag so the laugh carries it.

Comedy practice (sketch buttons)

The receipt

Hard proof on screen at the moment of doubt: the close-up, the real result, the real date.

HowShow it when the viewer's doubt peaks, not at the end.

Schwartz on verification; DR proof practice

Persuasion triggers

Persuasion triggers are why a story turns into a click. Each row says how far to push and where the line is; the line is a guard, never a dial. A trigger used without its truth (a fake deadline, a fake crowd) is refused.

Narrative transportation

A viewer absorbed in a story argues back less and takes on its beliefs.

HowDeliver the claim inside the story, never as a break from it.

The lineThe claim inside the story must still be true.

Green & Brock 2000

Social proof

People do what people like them are doing.

HowShow a crowd of people like the viewer, in the viewer's world, doing it.

The lineReal numbers and real customers only.

Cialdini, Influence (1984)

Authority

We follow people who look like they know.

HowThe pro, the doctor, the elder who has seen it all: a role the viewer already trusts.

The lineNever a fake credential; an actor is a character, never claimed as a real expert.

Cialdini 1984

Scarcity

Things feel more valuable when they might run out.

HowTie it to a real moment: the season, the event, the real stock.

The lineNever a fake countdown or fake stock.

Cialdini 1984; Worchel et al. 1975

Loss aversion

A loss hurts about twice as much as the same gain feels good.

HowFrame what the viewer keeps losing: the photos deleted, the plans skipped.

The lineNo fear about a health problem the viewer does not have.

Kahneman & Tversky 1979

Commitment and consistency

After a small yes, a bigger yes is easier.

HowAsk a question the viewer answers yes to in their head, then another, then the click.

The lineNever trick anyone into a commitment.

Cialdini 1984; Freedman & Fraser 1966

Unity

We say yes to people who are 'us'.

HowSpeak as one of their own: their slang, their world, their references.

The lineNever fake membership in a real community or culture.

Cialdini, Pre-Suasion (2016)

The reason why

A request with a reason gets more yeses, even a thin reason.

HowAlways give the mechanism: why it works, in one line.

The lineThe reason must be true.

Langer, Blank & Chanowitz 1978

Contrast and anchoring

A thing is judged against what is shown next to it.

HowShow the expensive, painful alternative first, then ours.

The lineThe anchor must be a real alternative at a real price.

Tversky & Kahneman 1974

Future pacing

Let the viewer picture themselves after, in a specific moment.

HowOne exact future scene: the photo posted, the compliment received.

The lineNever promise a result the product does not give.

Persuasion practice; mental simulation research (Escalas 2004)

Identity shift

People act to match who they think they are.

HowSell who the viewer becomes, not the tool: 'the one who never hides anymore'.

The lineNever shame who the viewer is now.

Identity-based motivation (Oyserman 2009)

Reciprocity

We return favours, even small ones.

HowGive something real first: the tip, the free quiz result.

The lineA gift with no strings, really.

Cialdini 1984

The common enemy

A shared enemy bonds the viewer to whoever names it.

HowThe enemy is the problem or the old way, never a person.

The lineNever a real competitor by name or a group of people.

Persuasion practice; us-vs-them framing

The edge

Right up to the line, never over.

Push it

  • Exaggeration and absurdity everyone can see is a joke
  • Taboo-adjacent humour: near-curses, innuendo, 'pause'
  • Real public stories, told as what was publicly reported, allegations labelled
  • Real fears and real embarrassment, shown honestly
  • Lookalike archetypes and cultural nods, never the real person
  • Uncomfortable truths about the problem, the category and the old way

Never

  • A claim the product cannot back
  • A fake testimonial, review, crowd, expert or result presented as real
  • A fake deadline, fake stock or fake price
  • A real person named, impersonated or defamed
  • Mocking a health condition, disability, race, religion or any protected trait
  • Minors in anything risky
  • A real tragedy used for laughs
  • Shame aimed at the viewer instead of at the problem
