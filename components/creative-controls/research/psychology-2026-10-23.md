# Creative engine: frameworks behind each control

Verification key: **[V]** = confirmed in a web search this session; **[M]** = cited from memory, not re-verified (check before quoting externally). Where a scale anchor is my own operationalisation of a framework (not something the paper defines), it says "operationalised".

Default stance the owner wants: absurd, maxed out, hyper-specific. Scales below are the dials; the "default" suggestion at the end of each control is a recommendation, not research.

---

## 1. Valence (emotional direction), scale -2 to +2

**(a) Framework.** Russell's circumplex model of affect (1980): affect sits on two bipolar axes, valence (pleasant to unpleasant) and activation (arousal). Mehrabian & Russell's PAD (1974) adds a third axis, Dominance (in control vs controlled); Russell later moved dominance into appraisal. [V]

**(b) Scale.**
- -2 Aversive: unpleasant, threatening or repulsive (the problem at its worst).
- -1 Uneasy: mildly unpleasant, tension or discomfort.
- 0 Neutral: informational, no affective push.
- +1 Pleasant: warm, light, relieved.
- +2 Euphoric: strongly pleasant, triumphant, delighted.
- Optional third axis (PAD Dominance, 0 to 4): 0 = victim, helpless; 4 = in total command. Useful for "problem" vs "solution" beats.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: valence lives in word choice and sentence frame (loss vs gain, "still hiding" vs "finally free").
- Video: valence is carried by faces, music mode/tempo, colour grade, and the arc (low-valence open to high-valence close).
- Static: valence is carried by facial expression, colour temperature and light, and composition (cramped/dark vs open/bright).

**(d) Citations.**
- Russell, J. A. (1980). A circumplex model of affect. *J. Personality and Social Psychology* 39(6), 1161-1178. https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/others/1980/russell1980a.pdf
- Mehrabian, A., & Russell, J. A. (1974). *An Approach to Environmental Psychology*. MIT Press. [M]

Note: for DR the usual shape is negative-to-positive (problem at -2/-1, payoff at +1/+2). Score valence per beat, not per ad.

## 2. Intensity / arousal, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Same circumplex, activation axis; plus Berlyne's arousal-potential theory (collative properties: novelty, complexity, surprise, incongruity raise arousal; hedonic value follows an inverted U). Berger & Milkman (2012) show activation, not valence alone, drives sharing. [V for B&M; M for Berlyne]

**(b) Scale.**
- 0 Deactivated: calm, flat, sleepy (sadness, contentment).
- 1 Low: mild, conversational.
- 2 Moderate: engaged, alert.
- 3 High: heart-rate-up (anger, anxiety, awe, amusement bursts).
- 4 Max: peak activation, shock, scream, can't-look-away.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: sentence length, punctuation, caps, verbs, stakes.
- Video: cut rate, camera motion, audio loudness/pitch, performer energy.
- Static: contrast, saturation, scale of the subject, crop tightness, motion implied.

**(d) Citations.**
- Berger, J., & Milkman, K. L. (2012). What makes online content viral? *J. Marketing Research* 49(2), 192-205. https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf
- Berlyne, D. E. (1971). *Aesthetics and Psychobiology*. Appleton-Century-Crofts. [M]

Caveat: Berger & Milkman is about sharing of NYT articles, not conversion. Berlyne's inverted U means 4 can overshoot; the engine should expect 3 vs 4 to be a testable pair.

## 3. Emotion (named, discrete)

**(a) Framework.** Three competing taxonomies. Ekman: 6 basic (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise; universal faces). Plutchik: 8 primaries on a wheel with intensity levels (e.g. annoyance, anger, rage) and blends. Cowen & Keltner (2017): self-report of 2,185 clips needed 27 categories, bridged by gradients, and discrete categories (e.g. amusement) predicted experience better than valence/arousal ratings. Critique exists (Barrett et al. 2018, TICS). [V]

**(b) Scale.** Treat as a categorical pick plus a 0-4 intensity (Plutchik's intensity ladder). Each emotion gets: 0 absent, 1 trace, 2 clear, 3 strong, 4 overwhelming. Use ONE primary emotion per beat, at most one secondary.

Cowen & Keltner's 27: admiration, adoration, aesthetic appreciation, amusement, anger, anxiety, awe, awkwardness, boredom, calmness, confusion, craving, disgust, empathic pain, entrancement, envy, excitement, fear, horror, interest, joy, nostalgia, relief, romance, sadness, satisfaction, surprise. [M for the exact list; V for "27"]

**Recommended shortlist of 10 for men with a skin/grooming problem** (my selection, mapped to the taxonomy):
1. Disgust (Ekman; Cowen "disgust"): the problem as repulsive. Morales & Fitzsimons show disgust transfers by contact (product contagion), so keep it on the problem, never near the product. Morales, Wu & Fitzsimons (2012) found disgust can strengthen fear appeals. [V citations; effect strength unchecked]
2. Embarrassment / awkwardness (Cowen "awkwardness"; self-conscious emotion): being seen with the problem. Dahl, Manchanda & Argo (2001) is the ad/consumer reference on embarrassment. [M]
3. Anger / frustration (Plutchik annoyance to rage): "tried everything, nothing works", the failed-solutions beat.
4. Anxiety (Cowen "anxiety"): fear of judgement, of dates, of the barber noticing.
5. Empathic pain (Cowen): the wince at razor burn or an ingrown hair; high-arousal, negative, very visual.
6. Amusement (Cowen): releases the tension, enables shareability (see humor, control 5).
7. Surprise (Ekman/Plutchik): the pattern-interrupt opener.
8. Relief (Cowen): problem ends; the cleanest payoff emotion after aversive beats.
9. Satisfaction / craving (Cowen): visual proof, the "satisfying" result; also craving for the outcome.
10. Pride / confidence (not in Cowen's 27; Tracy & Robins self-conscious emotions [M]; Plutchik joy-to-ecstasy): end state, "walks in the room differently". Alternative if you want to stay inside the 27: admiration/envy (other men's reaction).

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: name the felt state and the trigger situation ("the barber pauses"); specific sensory words carry disgust and pain.
- Video: facial micro-expression, reaction shots, sound design (sharp inhale), pacing.
- Static: face and body language dominate; one clear expression beats a mixed one.

**(d) Citations.**
- Cowen, A. S., & Keltner, D. (2017). Self-report captures 27 distinct categories of emotion bridged by continuous gradients. *PNAS* 114(38), E7900-E7909. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5617253/
- Morales, A. C., & Fitzsimons, G. J. (2007). Product contagion: changing consumer evaluations through physical contact with "disgusting" products. *J. Marketing Research* 44(2), 272-283. https://people.duke.edu/~gavan/bio/GJF_articles/contagion_jmr_07.pdf
- Ekman (1992) "An argument for basic emotions"; Plutchik (1980) *Emotion: A Psychoevolutionary Synthesis*. [M]

## 4. Absurdity / incongruity, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Mandler's schema-incongruity theory (1982): moderate mismatch with an expected schema draws processing and, if resolvable, delight; extreme mismatch that can't be resolved produces frustration. Meyers-Levy & Tybout (1989) tested it for products (inverted U). Heckler & Childers (1992) split incongruity into two dimensions, expectancy (is it unexpected?) and relevancy (does it relate to the message?); only relevant-and-unexpected material helps memory for the message. [V for H&C and M-L&T; M for Mandler primary]

**(b) Scale (operationalised from the expectancy x relevancy grid).**
- 0 Expected + relevant: category-standard execution.
- 1 Mild twist: unexpected detail, still inside the category's schema.
- 2 Clear incongruity, resolved: an odd premise that the ad explains in-beat.
- 3 Strong absurdity, relevant: impossible/surreal scenario that metaphorically IS the product benefit (relevant + unexpected).
- 4 Maximal: surreal, rule-breaking, dream logic, but still tied to the claim. Never "unexpected + irrelevant" (the H&C quadrant that hurts message memory).

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: impossible comparisons, absurd specifics, non-sequitur resolved by the payoff line.
- Video: a scene that breaks physical/social rules (giant objects, wrong setting, deadpan wrongness) with a clean resolution beat.
- Static: surreal juxtaposition, scale distortion, one impossible object in an otherwise real photo.

**(d) Citations.**
- Heckler, S. E., & Childers, T. L. (1992). The role of expectancy and relevancy in memory for verbal and visual information: what is incongruency? *J. Consumer Research* 18(4), 475-492. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v18y1992i4p475-92.html
- Meyers-Levy, J., & Tybout, A. M. (1989). Schema congruity as a basis for product evaluation. *J. Consumer Research* 16(1), 39-54. https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1989/schema-congruity-as-a-basis-for-product-evaluation/

Cautions: (1) moderate incongruity effect is contested; a pre-registered replication set (Gao, de Hooge & Fischer) found no effect, and the benefit shrinks for experts in a category. (2) Pieters, Warlop & Wedel (2002, Management Science 48(6), 765-781) found originality grabs attention but can pull attention to the ad and away from the brand: https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/breaking-through-the-clutter-benefits-of-advertisement-originalit/ [V]. So the engine should score "absurdity at 3-4 AND brand/product/claim still legible" as a pair.

## 5. Humor, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Benign violation theory (McGraw & Warren 2010): humor occurs when something is simultaneously a violation (threatens norms/identity/expectation) and benign (alternative norm, weak commitment, or psychological distance). Speck's humorous message taxonomy (1991): five types (comic wit, sentimental humor, satire, sentimental comedy, full comedy) built on three processes (incongruity-resolution, disparagement, arousal-safety). [V]

**(b) Scale (operationalised).**
- 0 None.
- 1 Wry: light wit, a smile (Speck: comic wit).
- 2 Playful: clearly jokey, warm (sentimental humor).
- 3 Comic with a target or edge: disparagement/satire, a sharp violation made safe by distance or a villain.
- 4 Full comedy: sustained gag structure, violation and benign-ness both high (Speck: full comedy; arousal-safety + incongruity-resolution). Failure mode: violation without benign-ness reads as offensive.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: setup/punchline, rule of three, deadpan specifics, self-deprecation.
- Video: timing, performer, reaction shot, escalation; the funniest medium for the benign-violation structure.
- Static: visual pun or single-frame gag; caption carries the punch.

**(d) Citations.**
- McGraw, A. P., & Warren, C. (2010). Benign violations: making immoral behavior funny. *Psychological Science* 21(8), 1141-1149. https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf
- Speck, P. S. (1991). The humorous message taxonomy: a framework for the study of humorous ads. *Current Issues and Research in Advertising* 13, 1-44. DOI 10.1080/01633392.1991.10504957

Fit note for this audience: the problem topic is naturally a violation (disgust, embarrassment). Humor is the tool that makes it benign; BVT predicts that distance (exaggeration, a character, a cartoon logic) is what does that. A 2023 EJHR study of audience perception clusters ad humour into taboo-breaking, exaggeration and cute/sentimental, which maps well to this brand. https://europeanjournalofhumour.org/ejhr/article/view/781 [V]

## 6. Specificity / vividness / concreteness, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Vividness effect (Nisbett & Ross 1980): concrete, emotionally interesting, imagery-provoking information outweighs abstract information in judgment. Paivio's dual-coding theory: concrete words/pictures get both verbal and imagery codes, so are remembered better. MacInnis & Price (1987): imagery as a processing mode that represents sensory information in working memory and influences evaluation and choice. Caveat: the vividness effect is elusive in meta-analysis (Blondé & Girandola 2016). [V for N&R, MacInnis & Price, Blondé; M for Paivio]

**(b) Scale (operationalised).**
- 0 Abstract claim ("better skin").
- 1 Category-concrete ("fewer breakouts").
- 2 Concrete image: one sensory detail or one named situation.
- 3 Hyper-specific: named place, number, time, object, body part ("the left side of your jaw, 6:40am, the bathroom light that shows everything").
- 4 Multi-sense scene: two or more senses, a precise moment and a precise number or object, a single-frame mental movie.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: nouns over adjectives, numbers, sensory verbs; specific beats general.
- Video: macro/close-up on the exact detail, real texture, on-screen proof moment.
- Static: single concrete subject, real detail visible (pores, skin texture, product on a real shelf), not stock.

**(d) Citations.**
- Nisbett, R. E., & Ross, L. (1980). *Human Inference: Strategies and Shortcomings of Social Judgment*. Prentice-Hall. (summary in Smith & Shaffer 2000: https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%207/SmithShaffer2000.pdf)
- MacInnis, D. J., & Price, L. L. (1987). The role of imagery in information processing: review and extensions. *J. Consumer Research* 13(4), 473-491. https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/macinnis/intellcont/4656740-1.pdf
- Also: Paivio, A. (1986). *Mental Representations: A Dual Coding Approach*. Oxford. [M]

## 7. Dramatisation / exaggeration, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** No single canonical theory; three overlapping bodies: (i) puffery/hyperbole research (Xu & Wyer 2010, JCR: puffery lifts evaluations of less-informed consumers, backfires with well-informed ones; later work finds belief and influence diverge); (ii) figurative-language persuasion (Kronrod & Danziger 2013: figurative/hyperbolic language helps hedonic, not utilitarian, consumption); (iii) the demonstration tradition ("show don't tell"; Ogilvy/Hopkins lineage, and demonstration ads in consumer research) where exaggerating the visible effect is the drama. [V for K&D; Xu & Wyer from a search summary, details unchecked; demonstration lineage M]

**(b) Scale (operationalised).**
- 0 Literal: states the fact.
- 1 Mild emphasis: strong adjective, still plausible.
- 2 Dramatised: acted reaction, exaggerated before/after, still physically possible.
- 3 Hyperbole: physically impossible but obviously figurative ("my face could stop traffic").
- 4 Heightened-reality demo: the effect is staged in the extreme (a visible, impossible, instant result) and shown, not said. The "obviously not literal" cue is what protects credibility.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: hyperbole, extreme comparison, specific consequence.
- Video: staged reaction, speed-ramp, dramatic reveal, exaggerated demo of the effect.
- Static: oversized product or problem, extreme before/after, strong expression.

**(d) Citations.**
- Kronrod, A., & Danziger, S. (2013). "Wii will rock you!" The use and effect of figurative language in consumer reviews of hedonic and utilitarian consumption. *J. Consumer Research* 40(4), 726-739. https://en-coller.tau.ac.il/sites/coller-english.tau.ac.il/files/RP_243-Danziger.pdf
- Xu, A. J., & Wyer, R. S. (2010). Puffery in advertisements: the effects of media context, communication norms, and consumer knowledge. *J. Consumer Research* 37(2). [M, see summary https://news.illinois.edu/head-scratching-ad-claims-can-alienate-consumers-study-finds/]

Regulatory flag (not research): exaggeration in health/skin claims is where Meta policy and FTC substantiation bite. Keep hyperbole on emotion and premise (obviously non-literal), not on claimed medical outcomes.

## 8. Native-ness / ad-likeness, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Persuasion Knowledge Model (Friestad & Wright 1994): consumers hold knowledge about persuasion agents' goals and tactics; once triggered ("this is an ad") they cope by discounting, resisting or counter-arguing. Native-advertising recognition research operationalises it: when ads look like organic content, recognition is low and evaluation is not yet penalised; once recognised, evaluations of ad and brand drop. [V]

**(b) Scale (operationalised; 0 = most native).**
- 0 Fully native: indistinguishable from organic content, no ad cues.
- 1 Native with brand present: organic form, brand/product shown but not pitched.
- 2 Hybrid: creator-style format with an explicit offer or CTA.
- 3 Ad-coded: recognisable ad structure (product hero, claims, price, CTA).
- 4 Fully ad: broadcast/brand-commercial polish, logo-forward.

Name the control "ad-likeness" with 0 = native, so up = more advertising. Persuasion-knowledge triggers to track: logo early, offer language, music bed, polished lighting, direct address "buy".

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: first-person voice, no headline claim, story-first vs offer-first.
- Video: handheld/selfie framing, platform-native pacing and captions, no title card.
- Static: looks like a screenshot, note, meme or organic post; no CTA button graphic.

**(d) Citations.**
- Friestad, M., & Wright, P. (1994). The persuasion knowledge model: how people cope with persuasion attempts. *J. Consumer Research* 21(1), 1-31. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v21y1994i1p1-31.html
- Wojdynski, B. W., & Evans, N. J. (2016). Going native: effects of disclosure position and language on the recognition and evaluation of online native advertising. *J. Advertising* 45(2), 157-168. DOI 10.1080/00913367.2015.1115380 (recognition as ad was only ~7% in study 1; recognition lowered evaluations). [V]

Interaction note: platform policy requires paid-partnership disclosure on creator content, so true 0 is not always available; the dial is about FORM, not concealment.

## 9. Realism / authenticity, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Consumer authenticity research: authenticity is something the consumer attributes via cues (Beverland & Farrelly 2010: control, connection, virtue); Napoli et al. (2014) built a consumer-based brand-authenticity scale (heritage, sincerity, quality commitment). For UGC vs polished there is no strong consensus: head-to-head effects are small or mixed; authenticity does mediate trust and purchase intent when it is perceived. [V]

**(b) Scale (operationalised).**
- 0 Stylised/artificial: obviously constructed, cartoon, CGI, or staged hyper-real.
- 1 Polished studio: professional, believable but idealised.
- 2 Casual pro: natural light, real location, light edit.
- 3 Lo-fi real: phone-shot, imperfect framing, real skin, unscripted feel.
- 4 Documentary: unvarnished, verifiably real person/place/moment, raw.

Note: this axis is independent of absurdity (control 4). Absurd + lo-fi real (a real man, real bathroom, impossible event) is a distinct and testable combination; absurd + polished is the other. Keep them as separate dials.

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: voice (speech rhythm, hesitation, slang) and specifics; not marketing tone.
- Video: handheld, available light, real room, imperfect delivery, no stock.
- Static: real skin texture, unretouched, candid composition, phone EXIF look.

**(d) Citations.**
- Beverland, M. B., & Farrelly, F. J. (2010). The quest for authenticity in consumption: consumers' purposive choice of authentic cues to shape experienced outcomes. *J. Consumer Research* 36(5), 838-856. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v36y2010i5p838-856.html
- Napoli, J., Dickinson, S. J., Beverland, M. B., & Farrelly, F. (2014). Measuring consumer-based brand authenticity. *J. Business Research* 67(6). https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/measuring-consumer-based-brand-authenticity/

Evidence flag: vendor stats ("UGC converts 5x") have no methodology behind them; the academic comparisons are small. Treat the UGC advantage as a hypothesis for the engine's own A/B data.

## 10. Attention / stopping power, scale 0 to 4

**(a) Framework.** Three measurable strands. (i) Karen Nelson-Field / Amplified Intelligence: attention is measured (eye tracking, panels) as active attention seconds and attention quality; platform and creative both matter, and distinctive brand assets can register in as little as ~1.5 s of active attention (VCCP/Nelson-Field "Hacking the Attention Economy"). (ii) Lumen Research: gaze-based "attention seconds"; PwC's review found Lumen's model predicted viewed/not-viewed about 70% of the time and tied attention to clicks and conversions better than viewability did. (iii) Ehrenberg-Bass (Romaniuk): distinctive brand assets scored on fame and uniqueness, so the stopping image also carries brand. Pattern-interrupt in the creative sense maps to Berlyne's collative variables and Pieters et al.'s originality. [V for strands i-iii as reported; primary studies not read]

**(b) Scale (operationalised from the above).**
- 0 Wallpaper: category-standard, nothing to catch the eye.
- 1 Noticeable: one salient element (colour, face, motion).
- 2 Stops a scroll sometimes: clear visual or verbal hook within the first second.
- 3 Strong interrupt: unexpected image/sound in frame one, face or action competing for the eye, brand cue present early.
- 4 Hard stop: unmissable pattern break in the first 1-1.5 s AND a distinctive brand asset visible, AND a reason to stay (open loop). Score the hook and the brand legibility separately (Pieters: attention to ad can steal from brand).

**(c) By medium.**
- Copy: first line (pattern break, specific, open loop); in-feed, the first ~5 words decide the scroll.
- Video: frame one and first 1-3 s: motion, face, unusual image, sound; thumbstop rate is the in-platform proxy.
- Static: one dominant focal point, high contrast against the feed, a face or odd object; text kept to what reads in a glance.

**(d) Citations.**
- Nelson-Field, K. (2020). *The Attention Economy and How Media Works: Simple Truths for Marketers*. Palgrave Macmillan. Also VCCP Media/Nelson-Field "Hacking the Attention Economy". Coverage: https://www.marketingweek.com/attention-hacking-creative-media/
- Lumen: PwC review (2023) via ExchangeWire https://exchangewire.com/?p=1744049 ; Adnami x Lumen high-impact formats (2022) https://www.exchangewire.com/blog/2022/05/10/eye-tracking-study-sees-high-impact-formats-outperform-standard-display/
- Pieters, R., Warlop, L., & Wedel, M. (2002). Breaking through the clutter. *Management Science* 48(6), 765-781. https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/breaking-through-the-clutter-benefits-of-advertisement-originalit/
- Romaniuk, J. (2018). *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*. Oxford UP. [V as reported]

Caveats: attention-seconds link to recall is weaker than to clicks (PwC); Nelson-Field's "platform matters more than creative" is a reported talk claim, not peer-reviewed.

## How the controls interact (5 lines)

1. Berger & Milkman (2012): high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety, and positive high-arousal such as amusement) increase sharing; low-arousal (sadness) decreases it, controlling for surprise, interest and utility. So arousal should be set high regardless of valence; valence picks the emotion, arousal drives the spread.
2. Absurdity: Mandler / Meyers-Levy & Tybout predict an inverted U (moderate best), Heckler & Childers say only relevant-and-unexpected helps message memory, Pieters et al. say originality can steal attention from the brand; so "maximal absurdity" needs a relevance constraint and a brand-legibility check to be safe, and it is contested whether the inverted U replicates.
3. Humor needs a violation to be benign: BVT means disgust/embarrassment (violations) plus distance/absurdity (benign-ness) yields amusement; without the benign cue the same content reads as gross or offensive. Morales et al. (2012) also found disgust can amplify fear appeals, so a disgust-then-humor-then-relief arc is a defensible sequence.
4. Persuasion knowledge: more ad-like form (high ad-likeness) triggers discounting and counter-arguing; lo-fi/native form delays it, and absurdity plus humor can lower resistance by making the ad entertaining; no strong study isolates this interaction, so it is a hypothesis to test in the engine's own data.
5. Persuasion cost of exaggeration: hyperbole and puffery raise liking but can raise perceived deception, especially for informed buyers (Xu & Wyer 2010), and vivid/specific proof is the counterweight; pair dramatisation (7) with specificity (6) and realism (9) to keep it credible.
