# Creativity — a proven core, one new combination

Bound as `{creativity}`. Source: the doctrine component; refs are line pointers into the book, for verification only.

Creativity as combination — how a piece stays a format that already works and still comes out new. Every idea is a new combination of things that already exist; the craft is choosing which proven core to keep and which one or two things to combine into it. The tells layer removes the average; this layer replaces it with something the viewer has not seen in quite this way before.

**The master rule.** KEEP THE PROVEN CORE, COMBINE ONE OR TWO THINGS INTO IT. The work that lands hardest is mostly conventional with a small intrusion of the unusual — and people prefer what is novel and still recognisable. So the swipe's structure, its emotional map and its argument stay; the newness comes from one or two deliberate combinations the viewer can recognise both halves of.

**The one-line test.** Every combination can be said in one line: `<the proven thing>, but <the thing combined into it>`. A combination that needs a paragraph is two ideas or none.

**What this pass may change.** The creative pass changes how a piece looks, sounds and what it leans on — the look, the setting's dressing, the reference world, the delivery style, a B-roll face, the opening picture, the sound. A move that would change the structure — the format, the order, the roles, the argument, the emotional map — is printed as a PROPOSAL for the owner and never applied; the structure lock decides it.

_Every example here is illustrative — the shape of a move, never content for a brief. What fills a move comes from the swipe, the room and the brand files._

## 1. The rules

- **conventional-core** — Mostly conventional, a small intrusion of the unusual: one combination, two at most. Five new things at once is no format at all. _(research — uzzi-2013)_
- **recognisable-halves** — Most advanced, yet acceptable: the viewer must recognise both halves of a combination. Each half comes from the swipe, the culture read, the culture map or the bank, with its receipt; only the combination is ours. _(research — hekkert-2003)_
- **three-then-one** — Write three combinations, never one. The first is the average idea by construction. Pick the one that is furthest from what this brand has made and still instantly recognisable; print the other two as the alternates. _(research — young-1940)_
- **novelty-where-attention-is-won** — Put the combination where attention is decided — the opening picture, the setting, the look, the reference world. Never on the claim, the proof or the offer: those stay plain. _(craft)_
- **inside-the-remit** — A move inside the creative pass's remit is applied. A move on the format, order, roles, argument or emotional map is printed as a proposal for the owner and never applied. _(craft)_
- **new-against-what-we-made** — A combination is new against the brand's own work, not against the category: name what it differs from in what is already running, and leave the scoring to the creative-diversity check. _(craft)_
- **specific-not-strange** — Newness comes from a specific real thing in an unexpected place — never from randomness. A combination that is only strange is the generator's average in a costume. _(craft)_

## 3. The moves

### `one-axis-swap` — One-axis swap

- **What:** Keep everything and change exactly one axis on purpose.
- **How:** Pick the axis the audience will notice first — usually the setting or the look — and move it somewhere they know but have never seen this format in.
- **For example:** A routine filmed in a bathroom, moved to a car parked outside work at 7am.
- **Risk:** A swap the audience does not recognise is just a strange choice.
- **Source:** research — uzzi-2013

### `category-transplant` — Category transplant

- **What:** A format proven in another category, carried onto our product.
- **How:** The chain already does this when the swipe comes from another category: the structure is theirs, the product is ours, and the friction between them is the newness.
- **For example:** A cooking-video format used for a product that is not food — everything laid out, measured, combined.
- **Risk:** Carried without translation, the format's own category leaks through and confuses what is being sold.
- **Source:** research — young-1940

### `reference-collision` — Reference collision

- **What:** Two worlds the audience already knows, meeting where they never met.
- **How:** Take one reference from the culture read or culture map and play the piece in its frame — its narration, its camera, its music — while the content stays ours.
- **For example:** A nature-documentary narration over someone choosing what to wear.
- **Risk:** Works only when the room knows BOTH frames; a reference they don't share is noise.
- **Source:** research — koestler-1964

### `literal-metaphor` — The literal metaphor

- **What:** Stage the room's own phrase literally on screen.
- **How:** Find a phrase the avatar actually uses about the problem in the language bank, and film it as if it were true.
- **For example:** A room that says it is 'drowning in it' — shown as a person standing fully dressed in a slowly filling bath.
- **Risk:** A phrase the writer invented is not the room's; it has to be quoted from the bank.
- **Source:** craft

### `inversion` — Inversion

- **What:** Flip one expected role or order.
- **How:** The skeptic is the expert, the result comes first and the problem last, the product is the underdog — one flip, inside the swipe's roles.
- **For example:** The before-and-after told backwards: the result first, then the morning it started.
- **Risk:** An inversion of the structure is a proposal, not a creative-pass change.
- **Source:** craft

### `new-lens` — A new lens

- **What:** The same moment seen from somewhere unexpected.
- **How:** The mirror's view, the bystander's phone, the object on the shelf — the camera's position changes, the story does not.
- **For example:** The routine filmed from inside the bathroom cabinet as it opens and closes.
- **Risk:** A lens that hides the product or the face the beat depends on loses the beat.
- **Source:** craft

### `honest-exaggeration` — Honest exaggeration

- **What:** One true detail scaled up until it is funny or striking — the claim stays exactly true.
- **How:** Take a real, specific frustration and show it bigger than life; never exaggerate a result.
- **For example:** The pile of products that did not work, stacked to the ceiling.
- **Risk:** Exaggerating the product's result is a false claim; only the problem may be scaled.
- **Source:** craft

### `personified-problem` — The problem given a face

- **What:** The problem cast as a character — always the villain or the fool, never the customer.
- **How:** Governed by the humor rules in the delivery slice.
- **For example:** The problem as an uninvited houseguest who will not leave.
- **Risk:** A problem character the customer could read as themself turns the joke on them.
- **Source:** craft
